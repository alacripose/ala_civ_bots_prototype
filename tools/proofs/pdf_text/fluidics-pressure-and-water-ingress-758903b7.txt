1. The physical picture

"Fluidics" is the control and movement of fluids. At everyday scale it includes water in pipes, pumps, tanks, hoses, valves,
and channels. When the fluid meets a porous material, the same conservation laws still apply, but the path becomes a
huge network of microscopic channels.

Think of a material as a hidden plumbing network

A sponge, brick, soil bed, paper sheet, wood cell structure, or concrete paste can be represented as a connected set of
voids. Some voids are large; some are tiny. Some connect all the way through; some terminate. The flow rate is therefore
controlled by both driving force and hydraulic resistance.

Quantity  Meaning                 Typical role
p         fluid pressure          pushes fluid from high pressure toward low pressure
z         elevation               gravity changes pressure with height
rho       density                 sets hydrostatic pressure and inertia
mu        dynamic viscosity       sets viscous resistance
k         intrinsic permeability  how easily the material transmits fluid
n         porosity                how much void space exists
S         saturation              fraction of accessible pore volume filled by water
theta     contact angle           describes wetting of a solid by the liquid
r         effective pore radius   sets capillary pressure scale

Important: porosity alone does not tell you how fast water moves. Two materials can have similar void volume but very
different permeability because their pores differ in size, tortuosity, connectivity, and throat geometry.

2. Pressure in the real world

Pressure is force per area. In a still column of water, pressure increases with depth because the water above has weight.
Relative to a free surface exposed to atmosphere, gauge pressure is approximately:

                                                            p_g = rho g h

For water near room temperature, rho is about 1000 kg/m^3. A 1 m water column therefore corresponds to about 9.8 kPa
of gauge pressure. Ten meters corresponds to about 98 kPa.

Hydrostatic pressure  p(z) = p_surface + rho g (z_surface - z)

                      deeper points have larger liquid pressure in a static column

Hydraulic head        H = p/(rho g) + z

                      pressure head + elevation head; useful for groundwater and porous flow

Pressure gradient is what matters for flow

A reservoir can have high pressure and still produce little flow if the downstream boundary has nearly the same pressure,
or if the material resistance is enormous. Flow responds to the spatial gradient in the fluid potential, not to a single
pressure number in isolation.

For a one-dimensional saturated material, a useful model is:

                                                    q = -(k/mu) (dp/dx - rho g)

where q is Darcy flux (volumetric flow per cross-sectional area). If the material is horizontal, the gravity term drops out. In
vertical flow, gravity either helps or opposes the pressure-driven component.

Gauge vs absolute pressure

Gauge pressure uses the local atmosphere as the reference. Absolute pressure includes atmospheric pressure. For water
infiltration problems, using gauge pressure is often convenient as long as every boundary is expressed using the same
reference.

4. Capillary pressure: the "hidden pump" inside small pores

A curved water-air interface carries a pressure jump. For an idealized cylindrical pore, the Young-Laplace relation
becomes:

                                      Pc = p_air - p_water = 2 gamma cos(theta) / r

Here gamma is surface tension, theta is the contact angle measured through the liquid, and r is an effective pore radius.
For a wetting liquid with theta < 90 degrees, the liquid pressure can be lower than the air pressure while the liquid is still
pulled into the pore. This is the physical basis of capillary suction.

Capillary rise

If capillary suction balances the weight of a vertical liquid column, an idealized capillary rise is:

                                           h_cap = 2 gamma cos(theta) / (rho g r)

This reveals an unintuitive fact: a smaller pore can generate a larger pressure difference. That is why tiny pores can pull
water upward or laterally through porous solids.

Entry pressure and non-wetting liquids

For a non-wetting liquid (theta > 90 degrees), spontaneous entry is unfavorable in an ideal pore. An externally imposed
pressure can overcome the capillary barrier. In real porous materials, the actual threshold depends on pore-throat
geometry, surface heterogeneity, roughness, trapped gas, and wetting hysteresis.

Capillary pressure scale  Pc ~ 2 gamma |cos(theta)| / r

                          useful estimate for the pressure needed to overcome a pore-throat barrier

Example: a 10 micrometer pore

Taking gamma = 0.072 N/m, theta = 0 degrees, and r = 10 micrometers gives Pc about 14.4 kPa. That is equivalent to
roughly 1.47 m of water head. So a tiny wetting pore can develop a capillary pressure comparable to a substantial water
column.

5. Flow after water is inside: Darcy's law

Once a connected region of water exists, a porous material can often be modeled macroscopically as a hydraulic resistor.
For saturated, single-phase flow:

                                                   q = -(k/mu) (grad p - rho g)

In 1D horizontal form: q = -(k/mu) dp/dx.

The intrinsic permeability k has units of square meters and is a property of the material-fluid geometry. Hydraulic
conductivity K is often used in hydrology and folds in fluid density, gravity, and viscosity:

                                                 K = k rho g / mu

Then q = -K grad(H), where H is hydraulic head.

Why a crack changes everything

Permeability is highly sensitive to the size and connectivity of flow paths. A millimeter-scale connected crack can carry
water much more readily than a dense fine-pore matrix because the hydraulic resistance of a channel falls steeply as its
characteristic size increases. A material may therefore be "waterproof" through its intact matrix while leaking badly through
one connected defect.

For a flat slab with area A and thickness L, the saturated estimate is:

                                                   Q = (k A / mu) (Delta p / L)

This is a first-order real-world engineering model, not a universal law for every porous medium. It assumes steady,
single-phase, saturated flow with effective constant properties.

6. Unsaturated materials: pressure becomes a state variable

A dry material is not simply a saturated material with less water. As water content falls, air occupies more of the pore
network, water films get thinner, and the set of pores carrying liquid changes. Consequently, the effective hydraulic
conductivity can drop by orders of magnitude.

A common macroscopic state description uses water saturation S, water pressure p_w, and a retention relation connecting
saturation to capillary pressure. The broad structure is:

                                                Pc = p_air - p_water; S = S(Pc)

and then an unsaturated conductivity K(S) is supplied by a fitted constitutive relation. The well-known van Genuchten
model provides a closed-form water-retention relation and can be combined with conductivity models such as Mualem's.

Richards-type model

A standard continuum model for partially saturated flow combines water conservation with a constitutive relation for
pressure, saturation, and conductivity. The exact form depends on chosen variables, but conceptually it is:

                     storage change = divergence of [conductivity x hydraulic-head gradient]

This is the natural next step after Darcy: Darcy describes the flow law, while the unsaturated model adds the fact that
saturation and conductivity change as water content changes.

Hysteresis matters

The path by which a material becomes wet can differ from the path by which it dries. Pore-throat geometry and trapped
non-wetting fluid can cause different pressure-saturation relationships during drainage and imbibition. Do not assume one
curve works equally well in both directions.

7. Real materials: what the model predicts

Concrete and brick

These materials contain interconnected capillary pores at several scales. Dry concrete or brick can absorb water from a
wet face even without a large external pressure because capillary forces drive early uptake. Sorptivity is commonly used to
quantify the rate of capillary absorption. Initial moisture condition, pore structure, compaction, and mix composition all
affect the result.

Wood

Wood is anisotropic. Water can move through cell lumina and pits as capillary water and can also diffuse into cell walls.
Liquid transport is often much faster along the grain than across it because the dominant connected pathways are oriented
longitudinally. Thus a single isotropic permeability number can be a poor model for wood.

Soil

Soil is a classic unsaturated porous medium. Rain or irrigation first encounters a surface that may be dry. Water infiltrates
under a combination of gravity and capillary forces, while air is displaced. Hydraulic conductivity depends strongly on water
content. Fine-textured soils can hold strong matric suction but may have low conductivity when very dry.

Paper, cloth, and fibrous materials

Fibers form irregular pore networks. At low saturation, liquid can travel in thin films and bridges between fibers; at higher
saturation, larger connected flow paths develop. The classical Lucas-Washburn picture captures the square-root-of-time
scaling in idealized pores, while real fibrous structures can show more complicated transport.

Cracks, joints, fasteners, and interfaces

A defect can dominate total leakage. The matrix may have low k, but a connected crack, joint, or interface can provide a
much lower-resistance path. For practical modeling, treat these as separate hydraulic domains rather than averaging them
blindly into one bulk material property.

8. Worked numerical model

These examples are deliberately simple. Their purpose is to show how the parameters map onto physical behavior.

Example 1 - water pressure at depth

At h = 1.0 m, using rho = 1000 kg/m^3 and g = 9.81 m/s^2:

                                       p_g = rho g h = 1000 x 9.81 x 1.0 = 9.81 kPa

So a one-meter water column adds about 9.8 kPa of gauge pressure at its base.

Example 2 - capillary suction in a 10 micrometer pore

For gamma = 0.072 N/m, theta = 0 degrees, r = 10e-6 m:

                                          Pc = 2 gamma/r = 14,400 Pa = 14.4 kPa

Equivalent water head = Pc/(rho g) about 1.47 m.

Example 3 - pressure-driven flow through a slab

Suppose a slab has k = 1e-16 m^2, A = 0.01 m^2, L = 0.10 m, Delta p = 10 kPa, and water viscosity mu = 1e-3 Pa s. Then:

                               Q = (k A / mu)(Delta p/L) = 1.0e-10 m^3/s = 0.36 mL/hour

That tiny flow is why a low-permeability intact matrix can appear almost dry despite a large water reservoir pressing on one
side.

Example 4 - spontaneous capillary penetration

For an ideal 10 micrometer cylindrical pore with water, theta = 0 degrees, and t = 1 s, the Lucas-Washburn estimate gives:

                                 L^2 = gamma r cos(theta) t / (2 mu) -> L about 19 mm

Real porous materials usually deviate because pores vary in size, paths branch, air can become trapped, and the effective
flow geometry changes while the material wets.

9. A practical simulation recipe

A useful fluid-ingress model can be built from a small set of state variables. Start simple, then add complexity only when
the data demand it.

Minimum state

                                            driving force
p_w       water pressure                    gravity contribution
                                            controls which pores are water-filled
z         elevation                         resistance to saturated flow
                                            resistance under partial saturation
S         water saturation                  links pore occupancy to pressure
                                            entry-pressure estimate
k         permeability                      capillary sign and magnitude
                                            simple sharp-front approximation
K(S)      effective hydraulic conductivity  changes viscosity and surface tension

Pc(S)     capillary pressure

A simple timestep

1. Apply boundary conditions: outside water level, inlet pressure, drainage pressure, or humidity.
2. Compute capillary pressure from the local saturation or an effective pore radius.
3. Compute hydraulic head H = p/(rho g) + z.
4. Compute flux from Darcy-type flow using K(S) or k.
5. Update water content / saturation using mass conservation.
6. Recompute pore accessibility, conductivity, and capillary pressure.
7. Repeat until the desired time or equilibrium state is reached.

Pseudocode

for each timestep:
 boundary = get_boundary_conditions(t)
 for each material cell:
   Pc = retention_curve(S)
   Keff = conductivity_curve(S)
   H = p/(rho*g) + z
 flux = -Keff * grad(H)
 S = update_by_mass_balance(S, flux, dt)
 apply_sources_sinks_and_drainage()

Calibration order

Measure or estimate parameters in this order: geometry -> density/viscosity -> permeability or hydraulic conductivity ->
water-retention curve -> wetting/contact behavior -> defect pathways. This prevents one "mystery permeability" value from
being forced to compensate for incorrect geometry or missing capillary physics.

When the simple model fails

Upgrade to a pore-network model, multiphase formulation, fracture domain, or coupled heat/vapor model when you see
strong hysteresis, trapped air, phase change, preferential flow, swelling/shrinkage, chemical reactions, or flow that is
visibly concentrated in a small number of channels.

Parameter checklist for a real-world model

• Fluid: density rho, viscosity mu, surface tension gamma.
• Boundary: water pressure or water level, outlet pressure, temperature, exposure duration.
• Material: porosity n, permeability k or hydraulic conductivity K, effective pore-size scale, tortuosity/connectivity.
• Wetting: contact angle or a fitted capillary-pressure relation.
• State: initial saturation / moisture content and whether the material is drying, wetting, or cycling.
• Defects: cracks, joints, edges, interfaces, holes, fasteners, and preferential pathways.

Bottom line: a material does not have one universal "water pressure". Its response is the result of pressure and gravity
acting on a pore network whose hydraulic resistance changes with saturation, while capillary forces can actively pull water
into wetting pores. A good model therefore keeps pressure, geometry, saturation, and wetting as separate variables.
