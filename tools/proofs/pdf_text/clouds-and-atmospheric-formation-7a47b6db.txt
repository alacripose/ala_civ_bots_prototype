1. Where the water comes from

Earth's atmosphere is continuously supplied with water vapor. Evaporation from oceans, lakes, soil, and other surfaces is
the dominant surface-to-atmosphere pathway. Plants add vapor through transpiration, and ice and snow can also
contribute through sublimation. The hydrologic cycle continually moves water between the surface, atmosphere, clouds,
and precipitation. [4]

Water vapor is a gas, not a visible cloud

Individual water molecules are far too small to scatter enough visible light for us to see them. You can have very humid air
that looks perfectly clear. The visible transition begins when water molecules assemble into a condensed phase - a
population of tiny liquid droplets or ice crystals. [1,2]

Saturation and relative humidity

The atmosphere has a temperature-dependent equilibrium vapor pressure. In simplified terms, warm air can support more
water vapor before reaching saturation than cold air can. Relative humidity (RH) is commonly expressed as the actual
water-vapor partial pressure divided by the saturation vapor pressure at the same temperature, multiplied by 100%. RH
near 100% means the air is close to saturation.

   Important nuance: "the air can only hold so much water" is a useful teaching approximation, but it is not a literal
   container limit. Saturation describes thermodynamic equilibrium between water vapor and a condensed phase at
   a given temperature and pressure. Cloud microphysics also depends on particle size, composition, curvature,
   surface tension, and the dynamics of the air.

2. Why rising air cools and makes clouds

A very common route to cloud formation begins with upward motion. As an air parcel rises into lower pressure, it expands.
Expansion costs internal energy, so the parcel cools even if it exchanges little heat with its surroundings. This is the
adiabatic process. [1,5]

The dry and moist adiabatic lapse rates

For unsaturated air, the dry adiabatic lapse rate is about 9.8 C per kilometer. Once condensation begins, latent heat
released by condensation offsets part of the cooling, so the saturated (moist) adiabatic lapse rate is smaller and varies with
temperature and pressure, commonly on the order of about 4-7 C per kilometer in the lower atmosphere.

Atmospheric stability controls cloud shape

The surrounding temperature profile matters just as much as the parcel temperature. If the atmosphere is unstable, a rising
parcel can remain warmer and less dense than its surroundings and keep accelerating upward. That favors vertically
developed cumulus and, with sufficient moisture and instability, thunderstorms. A stable atmosphere resists vertical motion
and favors broad layers such as stratus or stratiform cloud.

   Key idea: cloud shape is a visible record of atmospheric motion. Flat layers often indicate widespread, gentle
   ascent or stable stratification. Towering clouds indicate strong localized updrafts and continued vertical
   development.

Cloud droplets generally do not appear efficiently by simply clustering pure water molecules in the open atmosphere. Most
cloud droplets form on pre-existing aerosol particles called cloud condensation nuclei (CCN). Sources include sea salt,
mineral dust, sulfate and other pollution-derived particles, smoke, organic material, and biological particles. [1,6]

Supersaturation is usually small - but crucial

Inside growing clouds, supersaturation with respect to liquid water is often only a fraction of one percent. That tiny excess
is nevertheless enough to activate an appropriate subset of aerosol particles. Updraft speed, temperature, aerosol
properties, and turbulent mixing all influence how much supersaturation develops and how many particles activate. [6-8]

Altitude is not the whole story

Clouds are three-dimensional and can span large vertical ranges. A cloud genus does not mean every part of the cloud sits
at one fixed altitude. In mountains, for example, a cloud can be classified as low-level relative to the local terrain even if its
elevation above sea level is considerable.

A useful visual rule

Cirro- usually signals high cloud; alto- signals middle-level cloud; strato- suggests a layer; cumulo- suggests vertical
heaps; and nimbo- is associated with precipitation. These name pieces are clues, not substitutes for the formal
classification system.

5. What is actually inside a cloud?

A cloud is not a giant blob of liquid water. It is a dilute suspension of enormous numbers of tiny hydrometeors - primarily
cloud droplets and/or ice crystals - moving with the air. Individual cloud droplets are typically on the order of several to a
few tens of micrometers in diameter, while precipitation particles are much larger.

Droplet growth: condensation

Freshly activated droplets grow by diffusion of water vapor toward their surfaces. Growth is fastest early on and slows as
droplets become larger because the available vapor field adjusts around each particle. Turbulence and mixing continuously
modify the local temperature and humidity histories that individual droplets experience. [5]

Droplet growth: collision and coalescence

In warm clouds, droplets do not all have the same size or fall speed. Larger droplets fall faster, overtake smaller ones,
collide, and sometimes merge. This collision-coalescence process can create droplets large enough to fall as drizzle or
rain. Turbulence can enhance collision rates under some conditions. [5,10]

Ice processes

Cloud ice can form through several pathways. Ice-nucleating particles can initiate heterogeneous freezing or deposition
under favorable conditions; at sufficiently cold temperatures, homogeneous freezing can occur. Once ice is present in
mixed-phase clouds, vapor tends to deposit efficiently onto ice because the equilibrium vapor pressure over ice is lower
than over supercooled liquid water. This can draw vapor away from droplets - the Wegener-Bergeron-Findeisen process -
while riming and aggregation can further grow ice particles. [3,11]

6. From cloud droplets to precipitation

Cloud droplets are small enough that their terminal fall speeds are modest and upward air motions can keep them
suspended. Precipitation requires a pathway that makes some particles substantially larger.

Warm-rain pathway

In liquid-only clouds, a broad droplet-size distribution allows faster-falling droplets to collide with slower droplets. If the
collision leads to coalescence, the drop grows. Repetition can move water from hundreds or thousands of cloud droplets
into a much smaller number of drizzle and raindrops. This is the classic collision-coalescence mechanism. [5,10]

Cold and mixed-phase pathway

In clouds containing both supercooled liquid water and ice, ice crystals can grow rapidly through vapor deposition. They
may then aggregate with other ice crystals or collect supercooled droplets by riming. The resulting particles can become
snowflakes, graupel, or hail. If they fall into warmer layers, they may melt into rain.

Why precipitation can shut the cloud down

Precipitation exports water from the cloud. Evaporation below or within the cloud can cool air and change buoyancy. Falling
hydrometeors can also collide with other particles and alter the distribution of liquid and ice. Thus precipitation is not merely
an output of a cloud; it can feed back on cloud dynamics and lifetime. [3,10]

Cloud streets and organized convection

When airflow and surface conditions are aligned, convection can self-organize into rows or bands. The spacing and
orientation of these structures reflect interactions among boundary-layer turbulence, buoyancy, wind shear, surface fluxes,
and cloud processes. The atmosphere is not merely a conveyor belt that lifts moist air; clouds can alter the air that
surrounds them by transporting heat and moisture and by producing precipitation.

   A cloud is a coupled fluid + thermodynamics + microphysics problem. The visible shape is the end product of
   processes acting across scales from molecular collisions to kilometers of organized weather.

8. Why clouds change shape so quickly

Clouds can look solid from a distance, yet their individual droplets are constantly forming, evaporating, growing, colliding,
freezing, and falling. Air is also being entrained from outside the cloud. These processes can change the cloud's
appearance on timescales of minutes.

Entrainment and mixing

Rising cloud air mixes with environmental air that may be warmer, colder, drier, or more humid. Dry-air entrainment tends
to increase evaporation and can weaken cloud water. Strong mixing can also broaden the distribution of droplet growth
histories. The balance between cloud ascent and entrainment is therefore central to cloud lifetime and precipitation
initiation. [5,11]

Cloud base can evaporate while the top grows

A growing cumulus may add condensate in its upper regions while droplets near the base evaporate in a drier environment.
What the observer sees is the combined result of these local gain and loss processes. This helps explain why clouds can
look as though they are boiling, billowing, or dissolving even when the larger-scale weather changes only gradually.

Fog is a cloud at the surface

The physics does not stop at the ground. When the air immediately above the surface reaches saturation and enough
condensed water becomes visible, the result is fog. Radiation fog, advection fog, upslope fog, and evaporation fog differ
mainly in how the air is cooled or moistened enough to reach saturation. [2]

Cloud lifetime is a balance

A useful conceptual equation is:

   cloud water change = formation + growth + transport - evaporation - precipitation removal - mixing
   losses

The terms are not independent: precipitation can alter temperature and humidity; latent heating can alter buoyancy;
aerosols can modify droplet number and size; and radiation can heat or cool cloud layers. That coupling is why clouds
remain an active area of atmospheric research. [3,8,12]

9. Why clouds matter for weather and climate

Clouds influence Earth's energy budget in competing ways. Sunlight can be reflected or scattered by cloud droplets and
ice, producing a cooling influence at the top of the atmosphere. At the same time, clouds can absorb and emit infrared
radiation, reducing the rate at which heat escapes to space and producing a warming influence, especially for high cold
clouds. The net effect depends on cloud altitude, thickness, particle properties, coverage, and the surrounding atmosphere.
[12]

Aerosol-cloud interactions

Changing the number, size, and composition of atmospheric aerosol particles can change cloud droplet number and size.
More numerous, smaller droplets can alter cloud reflectivity and can also change how efficiently cloud water becomes rain.
In ice and mixed-phase clouds, ice-nucleating particles can affect freezing, phase, precipitation, and radiative properties.
The sign and magnitude of these effects are not universal: they depend on the cloud regime, meteorology, aerosol
properties, and the processes operating at the time. [3,6,8,11]

   Why this is scientifically hard: cloud processes span many scales. Molecular-scale thermodynamics controls
   droplet activation; droplets interact through turbulence and gravity; clouds organize within weather systems; and
   those weather systems influence regional and global climate. Numerical models cannot directly resolve every
   scale, so many cloud processes must be represented through parameterizations.

What satellites can and cannot tell us

Satellites can map cloud cover, top temperatures, optical thickness, phase, and other properties over large areas. But
connecting those measurements to the underlying aerosol population, vertical motion, supersaturation, and individual
microphysical pathways is difficult. Combining satellites, aircraft, surface observations, laboratory measurements, and
models is therefore central to modern cloud research. [3,12]

Notes on terminology

Cloud condensation nuclei (CCN): aerosol particles that can activate into cloud droplets at a given supersaturation.
Ice-nucleating particles (INPs): atmospheric particles that promote ice formation under conditions where homogeneous
freezing would otherwise be unlikely. Supersaturation: water vapor abundance above the equilibrium saturation value.
Hydrometeor: a water or ice particle in the atmosphere, including cloud droplets, rain, snow, graupel, and hail. Latent
heat: energy absorbed or released during a phase change without a corresponding temperature change of the substance
undergoing that phase change.
