1. What the photograph most likely shows

The bright central disk appears consistent with the Moon, surrounded by a blue-white inner glow and a broader, low-contrast
gray-tan envelope. The rings have soft boundaries rather than a clean, sharply defined edge. That visual pattern is compatible
with a corona-like aureole produced by thin cloud, but a single processed photograph cannot uniquely identify the particles or
cloud layer.

Corona, aureole, or 22-degree halo?

A corona forms mainly through diffraction and interference as light passes through many tiny water droplets in a cloud. Droplet
size controls the angular pattern: smaller droplets generally produce a larger corona, while a relatively narrow droplet-size
distribution can make rings more distinct. Real clouds contain variable droplets and uneven optical depth, so a corona often
blurs into a pale aureole. [1]
A 22-degree halo forms by refraction through hexagonal ice crystals, commonly in high thin cirrus. Rays passing through faces
of the crystals are deflected by a minimum angle near 22 degrees, producing a much larger ring centered on the light source.
To identify one confidently, the ring should be measured in angular degrees—not simply pixels—because phone lens, zoom,
crop, and perspective change pixel size. [2]

2. How Colorado’s mountains reshape clouds and
precipitation

Colorado combines an inland, relatively dry climate with high elevation, broad plains, deep valleys, plateaus, and a north-south
mountain barrier. The Colorado Climate Center describes an average state elevation around 6,800 feet and sharp local
contrasts in temperature and precipitation. Elevation is a major influence, but slope orientation, wind direction, stability, and
moisture supply determine where cloud and precipitation actually develop. [5]

Orographic lift: the core mechanism

    1. Approach. Wind carries an air mass toward rising terrain. If the air can cross the barrier, the mountain forces some of it
    upward; in stable conditions, low-level air can also be blocked or diverted.
    2. Expand and cool. As pressure decreases with height, a rising air parcel expands and cools. If the parcel is unsaturated,
    its temperature initially falls at approximately the dry-adiabatic rate; once saturated, latent heat release changes the cooling
    rate.
    3. Condense. When the parcel reaches saturation, water vapor can condense onto aerosols into cloud droplets, or deposit
    onto ice in sufficiently cold conditions. Continued growth of droplets or ice particles is needed for precipitation to reach the
    ground.
    4. Descend and dry. On the lee side, sinking air is compressed and warms. Relative humidity often falls, and clouds may
    evaporate. This can contribute to a rain shadow, but the location and strength depend on the storm and wind profile. [4,9]

               Figure 2. Conceptual cross-section of windward ascent, lee-side descent, and mountain-wave cloud bands. Not to scale.

Why the effects vary over short distances

Precipitation generally increases with elevation in many Colorado mountain settings, especially during storms with strong cross-
ridge flow, but it does not increase uniformly everywhere. A sheltered valley can be drier than a nearby windward slope; a
leeward slope may be dry during a westerly storm yet receive precipitation when winds reverse. The timing and depth of
moisture are equally important. [5,9]

  Important distinction
  Mountains do not create water vapor or guarantee precipitation. They modify an existing air mass and storm. Lift can help
  clouds form only when moisture, temperature, and atmospheric stability support condensation.

Chinook winds are not just “hot air rolling downhill”

The simplest thermodynamic picture is compression warming during descent. Real Chinook episodes also depend on the
upstream air mass, cross-barrier wind, atmospheric stability, pressure differences, cloud/precipitation history, and whether
strong winds aloft mix down to the surface. The National Weather Service in Boulder identifies Chinook winds and pressure-
gradient events among the important cold-season high-wind mechanisms along the Front Range. [6]

Reading wave clouds

Wave-cloud bands are visible markers of rising and sinking branches in an atmospheric wave. In rising branches, air expands
and cools; cloud may appear if saturation is reached. In sinking branches, air warms and cloud can evaporate. A smooth cloud
line can therefore mark a wave even though individual air parcels move through it. The cloud shape alone does not reveal its
altitude, wave amplitude, or turbulence severity. [3,7]

What this means for the photograph

Colorado’s terrain can set up thin cloud, lens-shaped cloud, or wave bands under appropriate conditions. But the
photograph contains no clear terrain silhouette, cloud-motion sequence, wind observation, or calibrated field of view that
ties its particular glow to a mountain-wave event.

4. How mountain-driven motion reaches the upper atmosphere

The upper-atmosphere connection is chiefly dynamical, not a direct upward transport of ordinary cloud. When a stable air mass
flows across a mountain, it can be displaced vertically. Buoyancy acts as a restoring force, so the air oscillates and creates an
atmospheric gravity wave. These waves can spread upward and downwind from the terrain. “Gravity wave” here refers to
buoyancy-and-gravity restoring forces in a fluid, not gravitational waves in spacetime. [3,7]

The layers in context

Most clouds and nearly all ordinary weather occur in the troposphere. The tropopause—the boundary below the stratosphere—
is not at one fixed height; near mid-latitudes it often lies around 8–15 km and shifts with season and weather. The stratosphere
extends roughly to 50 km; above it lie the mesosphere and thermosphere. Layer boundaries are approximate. [8]

What a mountain wave can do above the peaks

  Carry energy and momentum upward. If the background wind and stability allow it, a wave generated near the ground can
     propagate into the stratosphere.

  Break or dissipate. As the atmosphere thins, wave amplitudes can grow; waves may steepen, break, or deposit momentum
     into the surrounding flow. This affects circulation and can generate turbulence, including in clear air. [7]

  Create wave clouds only where the thermodynamics cooperate. Cloud formation requires enough water vapor and cooling.
     A wave may exist even when the sky looks clear; upper layers can be dynamically affected without visible clouds.

Rare polar stratospheric clouds are a separate example of high-altitude cloud formation: they require exceptionally cold lower-
stratospheric conditions and are not a normal result of a Colorado mountain wave. The important point is that mountain waves
can affect air well above their source terrain; the clouds themselves usually remain in the troposphere. [8]

  Scale distinction
  A cloud ring in a night photograph is an optical pattern measured along the observer’s line of sight. A mountain wave is a
  three-dimensional airflow disturbance that must be inferred from cloud structure, winds, stability, or atmospheric
  measurements. They are different phenomena and one does not prove the other.

5. Integrating the photograph with Colorado meteorology

A plausible chain of events — with the uncertainty made explicit

    1. Weather sets the particles. A thin cloud, haze, or ice-cloud layer lies along the Moon’s line of sight. Terrain may
    influence the cloud indirectly through lift, wind, and atmospheric stability, depending on the local weather setup.
    2. Moonlight meets the layer. Droplets can diffract and interfere with the light, creating a corona; ice crystals can refract it
    into a halo. Scattering by haze or mixed cloud particles can produce a less structured aureole. [1,2]
    3. The camera records the result. Exposure and image processing can saturate the center and blur color or edge detail.
    The photo therefore contains both an atmospheric scene and a camera-rendered representation of it.
This sequence is physically plausible, but only the visual appearance is available here. Without the time, location, camera field
of view, cloud observations, and wind profile, it is not possible to determine which cloud type was present or whether terrain-
generated waves contributed.

How to document the next display scientifically

  Preserve the original file. Record the exact date/time, approximate location, viewing direction, phone model, lens/zoom, and any
     exposure settings; avoid editing the only copy.

  Measure angle, not pixels. Use a known lens field of view or an all-sky image to test whether the ring sits near 22 degrees. A
     classic halo is about 22 degrees in angular radius, not a fixed pixel radius.

  Record the surrounding sky. Note whether cloud bands are stationary, lens-shaped, or moving; check nearby observations,
     satellite imagery, and upper-air wind/stability profiles for the same time.

  Keep the hypothesis open. A compact corona, large ice halo, diffuse aureole, lens flare, or a mixture can look similar in a noisy,
     saturated low-light photograph.
