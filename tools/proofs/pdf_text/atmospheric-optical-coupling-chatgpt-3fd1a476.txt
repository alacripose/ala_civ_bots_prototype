Atmospheric Optical Coupling

Aerosols, Turbidity, Visibility, Clouds, Sky Radiance, and Twilight

A physics-oriented reference for real-time atmospheric appearance models

    Purpose. This document fills the atmospheric-optics gap between an existing cloud/CCN treatment
    and a renderer that already uses the Preetham daylight model. The central problem is to keep four
    different things distinct but coupled: aerosol optical depth and spectral slope; local extinction and
    visibility; cloud liquid water and cloud optical thickness; and the changing radiance/illuminance
    regime through twilight.

Primary literature used: Preetham, Shirley and Smits (1999); Bodhaine et al. (1999); Kasten and Young (1989);
Angstrom (1964); Horvath (1971); Brock et al. (2016); Spitschan et al. (2016); and related atmospheric-radiation
literature cited herein. Prepared October 2026. Equations are written for implementation as well as scientific
interpretation; approximations are explicitly labeled.

1 Executive synthesis

The most useful organizing principle is to avoid forcing one scalar to stand for all atmospheric obscuration.
Aerosols, molecules, clouds, and twilight all affect light, but they do so through different state variables
and at different spatial scales.

Core state separation

Aerosol loading        Column aerosol optical depth τa(λ); spectral slope α; local extinction

                       profile σext(z, λ).

Hygroscopic response Relative-humidity growth factor f (RH) that modifies hydrated

                       aerosol optical coefficients.

Cloud water            Cloud-base/top, in-cloud LWC(z), liquid water path LWP, droplet

                       size and cloud optical thickness τc.

Sky model              Sun position plus a clear-sky parameter such as Preetham turbidity;

                       use a separate cloud representation for clouds.

Twilight               Solar depression angle, with a dedicated transition in both intensity

                       and spectrum once the Sun approaches and passes the horizon.

A practical renderer can therefore follow the chain

   aerosol mass/size/composition → AOD(λ), α, ω0, g → σext(z, λ) → visibility and aerial perspective,
while clouds follow

              cloud thermodynamics → LWC(z), LWP, reff → τc → cloud scattering/attenuation.

Preetham turbidity is especially important because it is neither a modern synonym for AOD nor a cloud
parameter. In the original model it is a heuristic ratio between total and molecular optical thickness,
evaluated around 550 nm, and the original fitted clear-sky model is intended for a limited turbidity range.
The paper also explicitly notes that cloudy and partially cloudy density assumptions are outside the
clear-sky formulation. [1, 2]

2 Aerosol optical properties

2.1 Extinction, scattering, and absorption

The bulk aerosol extinction coefficient is the sum of scattering and absorption:

                                 σext(λ) = σsca(λ) + σabs(λ).

It has units of inverse length. In radiative-transfer work, this is the local quantity that determines how

quickly a beam loses direct intensity through the Beer-Lambert law,

                                                                   s

                                 I(s) = I0 exp − σext(l) dl .

                                                                 0

The single-scattering albedo is

                                 ω0(λ) =    σsca(λ) ,
                                            σsca(λ) + σabs(λ)

which separates mostly scattering aerosols from strongly absorbing aerosols. The angular distribution

of the scattered light is represented by a phase function; a common low-dimensional summary is the

asymmetry parameter g, the first angular moment of the phase function. NASA and NOAA aerosol

documentation treat AOD, ω0, and phase-function information as the essential pieces of a radiatively
complete aerosol description. [13, 6, 9]

2.2 Aerosol optical depth

Aerosol optical depth is the vertical integral of aerosol extinction from the surface to the top of atmosphere:

                                        TOA

                              AOD(λ) =         σext, a(z, λ) dz.

                                        z0

AOD is dimensionless because it integrates an inverse-length coefficient over length. It is therefore a
column property, while visibility is primarily a near-surface/path property.

AOD at 550 nm is a standard reference quantity in atmospheric observation products. NASA’s aerosol
documentation describes AOD as the vertically integrated aerosol extinction and uses 550 nm as a common
reference wavelength. [7, 8]

This distinction is fundamental for implementation: an AOD of 0.3 does not uniquely determine a
ground-level extinction coefficient. One must also know, or assume, the aerosol vertical distribution.

2.3 The Angstrom exponent

The classical Angstrom power law is
                                                                           λ −α

                                                    τa(λ) = τa(λ0) λ0 ,
where α is dimensionless and describes the spectral slope of optical depth. Equivalently, for two wavelengths,

                                                     α = − ln[τa(λ1)/τa(λ2)] .
                                                                  ln(λ1/λ2)

Large positive α generally indicates stronger short-wavelength weighting associated with smaller/finer
aerosol, while values near zero are associated with optically important coarse particles. NASA’s Dark
Target documentation gives the same qualitative interpretation and notes that typical operational retrievals
use visible or near-infrared wavelength pairs. [2, 8]

2.4 Why one AOD value is not enough for appearance

Two atmospheres can have the same AOD550 but noticeably different sky colors if their α differs. Two
atmospheres can also have similar AOD and α but different brightness patterns because their single-
scattering albedo, phase function, humidity response, or vertical distribution differs. A renderer that only
stores one scalar “haze amount” is therefore collapsing several physical degrees of freedom.
For a compact real-time representation, a particularly useful minimum is

                                                  {AOD550, α, ω0, g, Ha, RH},

where Ha is an aerosol scale-height parameter used to distribute column optical depth vertically.

3 Visibility and its relationship to extinction

3.1 Koschmieder relation

For a dark object viewed against a uniform horizon background under the assumptions of classical
meteorological visibility theory, the visual range is approximately

                              V=  3.912          ,

                                  σext

when σext is expressed in inverse kilometers and V in kilometers. The constant is associated with a 2
percent contrast threshold, since − ln(0.02) ≈ 3.912. The simple relationship is only valid under restricted
conditions; Horvath showed how non-uniform illumination, spatially varying extinction, object reflectance,
and observer threshold alter the result. [10, 11]

3.3 Molecular extinction must not be silently folded into aerosol AOD

Rayleigh optical depth is a molecular contribution and should be treated separately when a model wants
a physically interpretable aerosol state. Bodhaine et al. recommend computing Rayleigh optical depth
from the molecular-scattering physics and updated refractive-index information rather than absorbing
it into an empirical aerosol fit. At 550 nm, representative sea-level atmospheric Rayleigh optical depth
values are about 0.097, depending weakly on atmospheric profile and convention. [3, 5]

Thus a useful decomposition is

                                τtotal(λ) = τR(λ) + τa(λ) + τg(λ),

where τg covers absorbers such as ozone and other gases when needed.

4 From AOD and Angstrom exponent to Preetham turbidity

4.1 What Preetham turbidity means

Preetham et al. define turbidity as the ratio of the optical thickness of the total haze-plus-molecular
atmosphere to the optical thickness of the molecular atmosphere. In compact notation,

                                T = τR + τa = 1 + τa ,
                                   τR  τR

with the original optical application evaluating turbidity at approximately 550 nm. The paper uses
turbidity as a practical heuristic tied to atmospheric haze and visibility rather than as a complete aerosol
retrieval. [1]

The original daylight model uses Angstrom’s aerosol transmittance law in its direct-light attenuation
component and adopts a fixed wavelength exponent α = 1.3 for that part of the model. This is important:
the original Preetham implementation does not expose modern measurement-grade α as an independent
control. [1]

4.2 A first-order crosswalk
If a renderer has measured or modeled AOD550, a physically intelligible first-order mapping is

                                                         T ≈ 1 + AOD550 .
                                                                      τR,550

Using a representative τR,550 = 0.097, this gives:

The original fitted Preetham sky model is commonly used over a turbidity range of about T = 2 to 6.

5 Humidity and wet aerosol optics

5.1 Why water changes aerosol extinction

Hygroscopic aerosol takes up water as relative humidity rises. Particle size and refractive index change,
which can substantially increase scattering and therefore extinction. A common measurement-side quantity
is the scattering enhancement factor

                              fsca(RH) = σsca(RH) .
                                              σsca(RHref )

The literature shows that f (RH) is strongly dependent on aerosol chemistry, size distribution, and the
reference humidity. In measurements and models, it is often represented by an empirical power law or by
more physically based hygroscopic-growth formulations. [14, 15, 16, 17]

A simple engineering form is                    1 − RHref γ
                              fsca(RH) = 1 − RH ,

with RH expressed as a fraction and γ fitted to a specified aerosol type. This should be treated as a
parameterization, not a universal atmospheric constant. [14]

5.2 The renderer-friendly separation

For a compact model, it is better to write

                                          σext, wet ≈ σabs, dry + fsca(RH) σsca, dry,

than to multiply all cloud or atmospheric optical density by an arbitrary “wetness” factor. This preserves
the idea that humidity changes aerosol optical behavior, while a cloud water path is a different state
variable.
This is the physical interpretation most directly relevant to a parameter such as WET TURBIDITY GAIN:
that parameter can be understood as an intentionally simplified proxy for f (RH), provided its scope is
explicitly restricted to aerosol haze rather than cloud water.

6 Cloud liquid water content and liquid water path

6.1 Adiabatic in-cloud liquid water
For an idealized warm liquid cloud, rising air cools, saturation is maintained, and condensed liquid
accumulates above cloud base. In this document, LWC denotes liquid-water mass concentration in kg

                                                                    8
Atmospheric Optical Coupling  Research companion

m−3; meteorological literature also uses qL for a mixing ratio, so the units should be kept explicit. In the
adiabatic approximation, in-cloud liquid water content is approximately linear with height:

                                        LWC(z) ≈ fad Γad(z − zb), zb ≤ z ≤ zt,

where Γad is the adiabatic condensation gradient and fad is an optional sub-adiabatic factor. Ideal
adiabatic behavior corresponds to fad = 1; real clouds are often sub-adiabatic because of entrainment,
mixing, precipitation, and other processes. Observational and model studies routinely use the near-linear
vertical LWC behavior as an idealized cloud model. [20, 18, 19, 21]

6.4 Why LWP should not be substituted for aerosol turbidity

Although both aerosol water uptake and cloud water affect visible scattering, the scales and physics are
different. Sub-saturated aerosol water is a change in particle size/refractive index below saturation. A
cloud contains a concentrated population of liquid or ice hydrometeors in a saturated layer. A single
scalar water path should therefore not be inserted directly into an aerosol turbidity equation without an
explicit intermediate model.
A good implementation keeps

                                      {AOD, α, f (RH)} and {LWC, LWP, reff , τc}

separate, while allowing both to contribute to the final radiative transfer.

7 Sky radiance models and their validity domain

7.1 What Preetham is good at

Preetham, Shirley and Smits provide an inexpensive analytic approximation to daylight sky spectral
radiance and aerial perspective, parameterized by sun position and atmospheric conditions. Their model
is a fitted representation of atmospheric-scattering calculations rather than a full on-line radiative-transfer
solver. [1]
The model is therefore excellent as a real-time clear-sky baseline, particularly when the renderer needs a
single fast analytic evaluation instead of a multi-layer radiative-transfer calculation. The same paper also
discusses the established overcast-sky luminance formulation; that is a distinct sky-state model rather
than a volumetric cloud solution.

7.2 What it does not solve

The original paper states that its analytic formulas cover clear and overcast skies, while partially cloudy
skies were not presented as a solved case. The simulated atmosphere uses uniform or exponential-like
particle-density assumptions, and the authors explicitly note that those assumptions do not hold for

                                         10
Atmospheric Optical Coupling      Research companion

cloudy or partly cloudy skies. A renderer therefore still needs a separate cloud field and cloud optical
model for partially cloudy scenes. [1]
This gives a clean architectural boundary:

                  clear sky model + explicit cloud field + aerosol extinction/aerial perspective.

Do not treat a cloud as merely a very large Preetham turbidity value.

7.3 Hosek-Wilkie as a useful upgrade path

Hosek and Wilkie’s 2012 model preserves the analytic character of Preetham while improving high-turbidity
and sunset behavior, accounting for ground albedo, and treating spectral components independently. The
authors describe it as a drop-in replacement in many mathematical contexts. [23]
This suggests a pragmatic path: keep the physical state model independent of the sky evaluator. The
renderer can use Preetham now, while later substituting Hosek-Wilkie or a more modern sky-dome model
without changing how aerosol AOD, humidity, cloud LWP, and twilight are represented.

8 Twilight and the open-horizon ambient step

8.1 Twilight definitions

Civil twilight is conventionally the interval during which the geometric solar center moves from the horizon
to 6 degrees below it. Nautical twilight continues to 12 degrees, and astronomical twilight to 18 degrees.
The American Meteorological Society glossary also reports clear-evening horizontal illuminance decreasing
from roughly 585–410 lux at the beginning of civil twilight to about 3.5–2 lux at its end. [26]

8.2 Twilight is not just a dimmed daytime shader

Measurements show that twilight changes both intensity and spectrum with solar elevation. Spitschan et
al. measured visible irradiance through the civil, nautical, and astronomical regimes and found systematic
spectral changes, not merely a scalar decrease in brightness. More detailed radiative-transfer studies model
twilight as solar illumination scattered from a spherical atmosphere with altitude-dependent molecular
and aerosol structure. [25, 24]

For a first implementation, let d be solar depression in degrees, with d = 0 at the horizon and d = 6 at
the end of civil twilight. Solar geometry and optical air mass near the horizon should use a low-elevation
approximation such as Kasten–Young rather than a simple secant law. [4] Using representative midpoint
endpoints E0 = 500 lux and E6 = 2.75 lux, an engineering interpolation is

                                               E6 d/6  0 ≤ d ≤ 6.

This is intentionally labeled an engineering curve. Actual twilight illuminance depends on aerosol loading,
altitude, latitude, season, solar azimuth, cloud cover, ground reflectance, and light pollution. The
measurement literature supports using solar elevation as the principal coordinate but also demonstrates
substantial environmental variation. [25]

9.5 Radiative composition

At a high level, the path from the source to the visible scene can be represented as

τR +τa +τg                    cloud field                       ground + aerial perspective
sun spectrum −−−−−−→ clear sky radiance −−−−−−→ cloud-filtered radiance −−−−−−−−−−−−−−−−−→ camera radiance.

This is schematic rather than a replacement for a full multiple-scattering solver, but it gives each parameter
a clear physical home.

11 Failure modes this separation prevents

Failure 1: “More cloud” is implemented by raising turbidity. This makes the sky white/hazy
rather than volumetrically clouded and breaks the distinction between horizontal haze and vertically

                                                                   14
Atmospheric Optical Coupling      Research companion

concentrated cloud water.

Failure 2: AOD is converted directly to visibility with no aerosol profile. The resulting surface
extinction is implicitly assuming a scale height even if the code does not state one.

Failure 3: Wet aerosol is represented by adding cloud LWP. Sub-saturated aerosol water uptake
and saturated cloud droplets occupy very different microphysical regimes.

Failure 4: Preetham is extrapolated far past its fit range. The result may look plausible in one
camera direction while failing near the Sun, horizon, or during strong haze/sunset conditions. Hosek-Wilkie
is a better upgrade path for those cases. [23]

Failure 5: Twilight is just a smoothstep on daytime RGB. This misses the measured intensity
and spectral changes across the 0 to -6 degree civil-twilight interval. [25, 24]

12 Implementation checklist

A physically organized implementation can be tested in this order:

 1. Verify Rayleigh optical depth independently of aerosols.
 2. Verify AOD550 and α reproduce the intended aerosol spectral attenuation.
 3. Verify the aerosol profile integrates exactly to the requested AOD.
 4. Verify the visibility calculation is tied to local extinction, not directly to column AOD.
 5. Verify the Preetham turbidity crosswalk uses the same 550 nm and molecular-atmosphere convention

     throughout.
 6. Verify humidity changes aerosol scattering through f (RH) while leaving cloud LWP as an independent

     state.
 7. Verify adiabatic LWC integrates to the requested LWP and that fad changes water mass rather than

     merely color.
 8. Verify cloud optical thickness responds to both LWP and reff .
 9. Verify the clear-sky evaluator is not expected to synthesize actual cloud volumes.
10. Verify the open-horizon ambient step uses a twilight curve as solar elevation crosses 0 to -6 degrees.

Bottom line for the renderer
Use aerosol optical depth and the Angstrom exponent to control wavelength-dependent haze; use a
vertical aerosol profile to connect column AOD to visibility; use relative humidity to modify aerosol
scattering through a humidity response such as f (RH); use LWC/LWP and droplet radius for cloud
opacity; keep Preetham turbidity as a clear-sky parameter; and use a separate twilight transition
once the solar elevation enters the civil-twilight band.
