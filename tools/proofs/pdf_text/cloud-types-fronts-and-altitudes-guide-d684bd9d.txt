What this guide does: catalogues the ten internationally recognized cloud genera, their common species and notable visual
features; explains approximate cloud-base and cloud-top heights; and connects cloud structure to frontal weather systems. It is
designed to be useful for observing the real sky, not only memorizing names.

  One important rule: a cloud name describes what is observed. It does not, by itself, prove which weather system produced it.
  Height, shape, precipitation, motion, wind, pressure trend, and the wider synoptic pattern should be read together.

Reference basis: World Meteorological Organization (WMO) International Cloud Atlas; NOAA/National Weather Service educational material; Met Office
cloud guides; and peer-reviewed research on frontal cloud and precipitation organization.

1. How cloud height is described

A cloud has a base (its lowest visible level) and a top (its highest visible level). Cloud classification by level usually refers mainly to
where the cloud occurs or where its base lies, not to a hard ceiling. A shallow cloud can be low; a deep convective cloud can cross
low, middle, and high levels.

The WMO gives broad, region-dependent levels. For a temperate-region rule of thumb, high clouds often occur around 5-13 km,
middle clouds around 2-7 km, and low clouds from the surface to roughly 2 km. These ranges overlap intentionally. In the tropics,
high cloud tops can reach much higher; in polar regions, high-cloud levels are generally lower. The tropopause height changes with
latitude, season, and weather.

3. High clouds: Cirrus, Cirrostratus, Cirrocumulus

High clouds are commonly composed mainly of ice crystals because temperatures are very cold. They may look bright white in
daylight and can appear yellow, orange, or red near sunrise and sunset. They are often distant from the surface weather that
eventually reaches an observer.

CIRRUS (Ci) - fibres, wisps, hooks

Typical height: base often around 6-13 km (20,000-43,000 ft), depending on region and tropopause. Appearance: detached
streaks, filaments, feathers, curved hooks, dense fibrous patches. Physics: ice crystals grow by deposition from water vapour and
are shaped by winds and crystal habit. Weather clues: cirrus may be benign, may trail convection, or may thicken ahead of an
approaching frontal system; one isolated patch does not guarantee rain.

Common species: fibratus (fine threads, mostly straight or gently curved); uncinus (hooks or comma-like heads, often with a
trailing brush); spissatus (dense, thick patches); castellanus (turreted tops indicating vertical structure); floccus (small tufts with
ragged bases). Look for changes in coverage and direction over time.

CIRROSTRATUS (Cs) - high veil

Typical height: about 6-12 km (20,000-40,000 ft). Appearance: transparent, milky veil over part or most of the sky. The Sun or
Moon often remains visible. Ice crystals can refract light to produce halos, arcs, or bright spots. Weather clues: a spreading veil
that thickens and lowers can be a classic early sign of large-scale ascent ahead of a warm front or mid-latitude cyclone.

Common species: fibratus, a fibrous veil with threads; nebulosus, a more uniform, nebulous sheet. Cirrostratus can be so thin that
a halo is more obvious than the cloud itself.

CIRROCUMULUS (Cc) - very small ripples or grains

Typical height: about 6-12 km (20,000-40,000 ft). Appearance: tiny white cloudlets, grains, or ripples, usually with little or no
shading. It can form a “mackerel sky” texture. Common species: stratiformis (widespread sheet with elements), lenticularis
(lens-shaped), castellanus (small turrets), floccus (tufted elements). Weather clues: indicates structure or waves aloft, but is not a
stand-alone forecast of rain.

  High-cloud observation tip: note whether the cover is increasing, whether cloud elements are spreading into a veil, the direction
  of upper-level motion, and whether a halo forms. A trend over several hours is more informative than a single cloud photograph.

4. Middle clouds: Altocumulus, Altostratus, Nimbostratus

ALTOCUMULUS (Ac) - rolls, patches, or rounded elements

Typical layer: roughly 2-7 km (6,500-23,000 ft), with substantial variation. Appearance: rounded patches or rolls, often larger and
more shaded than cirrocumulus. Elements may be arranged in sheets or parallel bands. Composition: liquid droplets, ice crystals,
or both, depending on temperature and vertical depth.

Common species: stratiformis (cloudlets in a sheet); lenticularis (smooth lenses near mountain waves); castellanus (turrets rising
from a common base, evidence of elevated instability); floccus (tufted elements with ragged bases); volutus (roll-shaped cloud).
Notable features: virga may trail downward; cavum can appear as a circular or linear hole in a thin supercooled layer; asperitas
creates an undulating, rough underside; mamma forms pouch-like structures.

Weather clues: altocumulus castellanus in the morning can signal instability aloft and a possible risk of later showers or
thunderstorms if moisture and lift are sufficient. Lenticular altocumulus points to wave motion, not automatically to approaching
precipitation.

ALTOSTRATUS (As) - broad grey-blue sheet

Typical layer: about 2-7 km (6,500-23,000 ft); can extend higher. Appearance: widespread, grey or blue-grey layer; the Sun may
look like a dim disk through thinner areas. Usually lacks the sharp halo of cirrostratus. Weather clues: thickening altostratus can
mark deeper large-scale ascent and the transition toward nimbostratus, especially ahead of a warm front. Precipitation may be light,
intermittent, or still evaporating before it reaches the ground.

NIMBOSTRATUS (Ns) - thick precipitation layer

Typical base: often low to middle, roughly 0.5-3 km, but variable. Vertical span: commonly several kilometres, extending through
the middle layer and sometimes into low and high levels. Appearance: extensive, dark grey, fairly featureless cloud deck; Sun is
generally hidden. Weather: sustained rain or snow over a broad area is common. Nimbostratus is often associated with warm fronts
and occlusions, but also appears in other broad ascent regimes.

Important distinction: Nimbostratus tends to produce widespread, relatively continuous precipitation. Cumulonimbus produces
convective showers or storms with stronger local variability, and may bring lightning, hail, or damaging wind.

5. Low clouds: Stratus and Stratocumulus

STRATUS (St) - low, flat, grey layer

Typical base: at the surface to about 1 km (0-3,300 ft); may sit higher in dry or elevated settings. Appearance: a smooth, uniform
sheet, sometimes like lifted fog. It may give drizzle or fine snow, but often produces no measurable precipitation. Formation:
cooling of a moist layer, gentle lifting, advection over a colder surface, or fog lifting from the ground. The cloud base may be difficult
to distinguish from terrain in hills or mountains.

Common species: nebulosus (uniform fog-like layer); fractus (ragged shreds, often under precipitation clouds and sometimes
called scud in informal weather discussion). Stratus near the ground can reduce visibility substantially.

STRATOCUMULUS (Sc) - low rolls and cloud cells

Typical base: surface to about 2 km (0-6,500 ft). Appearance: low lumpy masses with broad elements, often in rows or sheets;
dark gaps or shading distinguish it from the more delicate high cloudlets. Formation: commonly the top of a moist boundary layer
cooled beneath an inversion; it may develop from a broken-up stratus deck, shallow convection, or air flowing over terrain.

Species: stratiformis (sheet); lenticularis (lens-shaped); castellanus (turrets); floccus (tufts); volutus (roll). Varieties/patterns:
undulatus (waves), radiatus (bands that appear to converge or diverge in perspective), perlucidus (gaps between elements),
opacus (opaque sheet). Asperitas and fluctus may add distinctive underside or wave structures.

What low clouds tell you: a persistent low deck often signals moisture trapped under a stable layer. A clearing trend after a front
may begin with broken stratocumulus, while renewed moist advection can rebuild the deck. Surface wind, temperature, dew point,
and visibility are especially useful because low clouds are close to the boundary layer.

  Fog: meteorologically, fog is a cloud at or very near the ground. Common forms include radiation fog (surface cooling on clear calm
  nights), advection fog (moist air moving over a colder surface), upslope fog (air cooled as it rises), and evaporation or steam fog
  (water vapour added to cold air). Fog type describes the formation process, not a new cloud genus.

6. Vertically developed clouds: Cumulus and
Cumulonimbus

CUMULUS (Cu) - detached heaps with a relatively flat base

Typical base: often 0.5-2.5 km, depending on moisture and surface heating. Appearance: cauliflower-like towers, bright sunlit
tops, and a flatter base near the level where rising air first reaches saturation. Mechanism: buoyant thermals rise from heated
surfaces or are lifted by terrain or convergence. If the air is stable or dry above, growth may stop.

Species and practical meaning: fractus - ragged, changing fragments, often in turbulent or precipitating environments; humilis -
small fair-weather puffs with limited vertical depth; mediocris - moderate vertical growth; congestus - tall cauliflower towers with
vigorous updrafts and potential for showers. Cumulus congestus can grow into cumulonimbus if the updraft and moisture sustain
deeper development.

CUMULONIMBUS (Cb) - thunderstorm cloud

Typical base: often around 0.5-2 km, but lower or higher bases occur. Top: may reach 8-16 km and often spreads near the
tropopause; strong storms can briefly overshoot it. The vertical extent is more important than a single “cloud height.”

Appearance and physics: deep convective tower with strong updrafts and downdrafts. Liquid droplets form in lower warm regions;
supercooled water, ice crystals, graupel, and hail may coexist in colder regions. The glaciated upper cloud can spread into an anvil
(incus) in strong upper-level flow. Precipitation loading and evaporation can help generate downdrafts and gust fronts.

Species: calvus - upper part mostly swollen and relatively smooth as glaciation begins; capillatus - fibrous or striated upper parts,
often including an anvil. Common features: incus (anvil), mamma (pouches), arcus (shelf-like or roll-like leading cloud), murus
(wall cloud), cauda (tail cloud), tuba (funnel-shaped feature), virga (precipitation that evaporates or sublimates before ground),
praecipitatio (precipitation reaching the ground). Accessory clouds can include pileus (smooth cap), velum (veil), pannus (ragged
shreds), and flumen (inflow band sometimes called a beaver tail in storm spotting).

  Safety note: an approaching cumulonimbus can produce lightning, hail, severe gusts, flash flooding, and tornadoes in some
  storms. Do not use cloud appearance alone to judge safe distance; thunder means lightning is close enough to be dangerous.

9. Warm fronts: broad ascent and layered cloud shields

A warm front advances when a warmer air mass replaces colder air at the surface. Because cold air is denser, the advancing warm
air usually cannot simply bulldoze underneath it; instead it glides up and over the shallow cold air along a gently sloping frontal
surface. The ascent can extend hundreds of kilometres ahead of the surface front.

Why the cloud layers form: as moist air ascends, pressure falls and the air expands. Expansion cools it; once it reaches
saturation, droplets and/or ice crystals form. Because uplift is broad and comparatively gradual, cloud tends to occupy a large shield
and may appear as successive layers at different heights.

Precipitation geography: the broad area of ascent and precipitation is commonly ahead of the surface warm front, on its cold side,

What changes at passage: temperatures and dew points often rise, winds shift, and the air mass becomes warmer or more humid.
Cloud cover may break in the warm sector, but stratus, stratocumulus, fog, or continued rain can persist if moisture and stability
remain.

  Warm-front pattern to remember: high cloud often arrives first; the deck thickens and lowers; steady precipitation becomes more
  likely as the cloud deepens. This sequence is a useful pattern, not a fixed timetable. Some warm fronts have little cloud or
  precipitation if the air is dry, ascent is weak, or the vertical structure differs.

A cold front is the leading boundary of colder air displacing warmer air. The dense cold air tends to wedge underneath the warm air.
The resulting lift is often more abrupt than along a warm front, especially when the front is strong and the warm sector is moist and
unstable.

Cloud and precipitation details: fast-moving cold fronts can produce a relatively narrow line of showers or storms. Slow fronts,

Post-frontal clouds: cold air moving over a relatively warm surface can create shallow convection, cloud streets, cumulus, or
stratocumulus. If the air mass is dry or subsidence is strong, the sky may clear rapidly. Over water, cold-air outbreaks can organize
striking bands of clouds and snow showers.

  Cold-front pattern to remember: look for a boundary, a change in wind and temperature, and a narrow or broken band of stronger

  terrain, and storm outflow can shift it.

Occlusion: in a mature mid-latitude cyclone, the faster-moving cold front may catch up with the warm front. The warm air is lifted
above the surface, and the surface pattern becomes an occluded front. The cloud field is often extensive and layered, but
embedded convection is possible.

Stationary fronts: repeated lift over a nearly fixed boundary can maintain cloudiness or precipitation. If waves develop along the
boundary, one section may intensify even while another weakens. A stationary front is a motion description, not a cloud type.

Frontal cloud systems in research: real cyclones contain multiple scales of cloud organization: broad frontal shields, narrow
rainbands, embedded convective cells, and post-frontal bands. The exact arrangement varies with vertical wind shear, stability,
moisture, topography, and the cyclone stage.

Cloud base is closely related to the lifting condensation level (LCL) for a rising near-surface parcel: a rough estimate can be
obtained from temperature-dew-point spread, but an observed base also depends on mixing, entrainment, and local structure. A
deep cumulonimbus may have a low base and a very high top; reporting it as simply “a 10 km cloud” hides this vertical range.

Cloud top is often tied to a stable layer: a capping inversion can flatten cumulus; a tropopause can stop or spread thunderstorm
outflow into an anvil; strong updrafts can penetrate briefly into the lower stratosphere. These are common patterns, not hard
geometric boundaries.

13. How clouds produce rain, snow, virga, and ice

Clouds are not simply water vapour made visible. Visible cloud is made of liquid droplets, ice crystals, or both. Water vapour is
invisible. A cloud forms when air reaches saturation and water condenses or deposits onto microscopic particles; later growth
processes determine whether precipitation reaches the ground.

Warm-cloud growth: in clouds above freezing, droplets collide and coalesce. Turbulence and the size distribution of droplets
influence how quickly some grow into drizzle or rain drops. Larger drops fall relative to smaller ones and can collect them.

Mixed-phase and cold-cloud growth: below 0 C, liquid droplets can remain supercooled. In many clouds, ice crystals grow at the
expense of supercooled droplets because saturation vapour pressure over ice is lower than over liquid water. Ice crystals may
aggregate, and riming can accrete supercooled droplets to form graupel or contribute to hail in strong updrafts.

Virga: precipitation falls out of a cloud but evaporates or sublimates before reaching the surface. A dry sub-cloud layer can
therefore show visible streaks under Altocumulus, Cumulus, or other clouds without rain measured on the ground. As precipitation
moistens the lower air, the streaks may eventually reach the surface.

Snow versus rain at the surface: the cloud can contain ice even when precipitation arrives as rain, if falling ice melts through a
warm layer. Conversely, snow can reach the ground when the vertical temperature profile stays cold enough. Freezing rain and
sleet depend on the depth and temperature of warm/cold layers aloft and near the surface - cloud type alone is insufficient to
diagnose them.

Frontal precipitation: warm fronts often have large regions of layered cloud and broad ascent, making sustained precipitation
likely. Cold fronts can contain convective rainbands where ascent is concentrated. Both may have embedded cells and both can
produce rain or snow, depending on the atmospheric column.

  Three observations to separate cloud from precipitation: (1) cloud form and vertical extent, (2) whether streaks reach the
  ground, and (3) the temperature profile between cloud and surface. A dark base alone does not identify precipitation type or
  intensity.

Fast recognition cues

- High wisps spreading into a veil with a halo: possible cirrus-to-cirrostratus progression; a frontal shield may be approaching.

- A grey sheet that gradually hides the Sun and deepens into steady precipitation: Altostratus progressing toward Nimbostratus is
one possible pattern.

- Growing towers with sharp cauliflower edges: strong vertical development; if they glaciate and spread an anvil, Cumulonimbus is
likely.

- Smooth lens clouds near mountains: likely standing-wave clouds; they can indicate strong winds aloft, but the cloud may remain
stationary relative to terrain.

- Low, widespread grey cloud with poor visibility: Stratus or fog; check surface temperature, dew point, wind, and terrain.

- A ragged low cloud under a storm base: could be pannus or scud; it should not automatically be interpreted as a funnel or tornado.

Do not overclaim: clouds can suggest processes, but a cloud photograph rarely proves a front location, storm severity, exact
altitude, or precipitation type. Use a current surface map, radar, satellite loop, and sounding when those distinctions matter.

One-minute summary

High clouds (Ci, Cs, Cc) are usually ice-rich and often reveal upper-level moisture, waves, or the leading edge of a broad cloud
shield. Middle clouds (Ac, As, Ns) expose the structure of moist layers and broad ascent; nimbostratus commonly supports
prolonged rain or snow. Low clouds (St, Sc) are often shaped by boundary-layer moisture and inversions. Cumulus and
cumulonimbus grow vertically when lift, moisture, and instability permit.

Warm fronts often feature a broad, gently ascending cloud shield and a possible progression from cirrus through cirrostratus and
altostratus to nimbostratus. Cold fronts often lift air more abruptly, allowing cumulus towers and cumulonimbus where moisture and
instability are available. Real cloud systems are messier: fronts can be dry, weak, stationary, occluded, or mixed with waves and
convection.

  Best field habit: identify the genus, estimate the cloud base and top separately, record how the cloud is changing, then relate it to
  wind, temperature, dew point, pressure trend, terrain, and current radar/satellite data. Always distinguish what you can see from
  what you infer.
