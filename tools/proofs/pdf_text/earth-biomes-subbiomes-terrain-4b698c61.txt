                        TERRAIN

How climate sets the broad envelope, while landform, water, soil, and geology carve that envelope into
                                                          real landscapes

 CORE IDEA

 A biome is not simply a place at a particular latitude. At broad scales, temperature and water availability strongly constrain
 vegetation. Terrain then redistributes heat, precipitation, runoff, snow, soil depth, and exposure over much smaller
 distances. The result is a nested landscape: broad biome -> regional ecoregion -> local habitat or sub-biome-like patch.

Latitude / oceans                                            Atmosphere                    Terrain
     energy input                                        temperature + rainfall  uplift + shade + drainage

   Soil / rock                                                Vegetation            Biome / sub-biome
water + nutrients                                        structure + feedbacks   recurring ecological pattern

Figure 1. A practical way to think about biome formation: global climate creates the outer envelope, but terrain and the soil-water system repeatedly
reshape conditions at finer scales. These feedbacks make neighboring landscapes ecologically different even under the same regional climate.

One important terminology note

"Biome" is a broad ecological category, usually defined by characteristic vegetation structure and climate. "Sub-biome" is
useful descriptive language but is not a single globally standardized scientific rank. For regional work, terms such as
ecoregion, ecological region, formation, vegetation type, and habitat are often more precise. The hierarchy used in this
guide is therefore conceptual rather than a claim that every classification system uses the same ranks.

1. THE FIRST FILTER: ENERGY AND WATER

Terrestrial biomes emerge from recurring combinations of heat, water availability, seasonality, and disturbance. Two simple
climate variables explain a surprising amount of the broad pattern: temperature and precipitation. But precipitation alone is
not the same thing as usable water. Wind, evaporation, snow storage, soil depth, drainage, and the length of the growing
season determine how much water plants can actually access.

A useful mental model is an atmospheric "budget":

Solar energy        Sets potential heating and growing-season energy; varies with latitude, season, cloudiness, and slope aspect.

Rain / snow         Adds water. Snow can also store water and delay its delivery into the soil until melt.

Evaporation +       Removes water from soil and vegetation; demand rises with energy, wind, and dryness.
transpiration

Runoff + drainage   Moves water away from a location, often making ridges and steep slopes much drier than nearby hollows.

This is why two places with the same annual rainfall can support very different vegetation. If one receives rain in a cool
season while the other receives it during a hot, high-evaporation season, their effective water balance can be radically
different. Climate classifications such as Köppen-Geiger formalize broad climatic regimes, while vegetation-based biome
maps add ecological structure. High-resolution climate maps show that sharp elevation gradients can produce rapid
transitions over short distances.

The Whittaker-style intuition

Imagine a graph with mean annual temperature on one axis and annual precipitation on the other. Forests occupy wetter
combinations, grasslands tend to appear where water is more limiting or seasonality favors grasses, and deserts occur
where atmospheric demand greatly exceeds water supply. Cold conditions create another filter: even with water present, low
temperature can limit growth and change which plants can persist. This is a climate envelope, not a rigid boundary.

3. TERRAIN IS A CLIMATE MODIFIER

Terrain does not replace regional climate; it redistributes it. A mountain ridge, canyon, plateau, dune field, river terrace, or
shaded slope can create local energy and water conditions that differ strongly from the surrounding landscape. The most
important terrain variables are elevation, slope, aspect, relief, and position relative to wind and water flow.

     Mountain terrain converts one regional climate into many local microclimates.

                                                                         alpine / subalpine

                                                         montane forest

                                                                                                  dry woodland / shrub / grassland

Windward: uplift -> cooling -> clouds/precipitation -> wetter soils                               Leeward: sinking air -> warming/drying -> rain shadow

Figure 2. A simplified mountain transect. Rising air cools as it expands, encouraging cloud formation and precipitation on the windward side. Descending air
on the leeward side is warmer and drier, creating a rain shadow. Elevation also lowers average temperature and changes snow duration, growing season,
and vegetation height.

Elevation: the vertical biome ladder

A mountain can compress climate zones that occur thousands of kilometers apart on a horizontal transect into a few
kilometers vertically. As elevation increases, air pressure decreases and the mean environmental temperature generally
falls. But the ecological transition is not controlled by temperature alone: wind exposure, snow redistribution, cloudiness, soil
development, growing-season length, and geology also change. Above a treeline, the system often shifts from forest to
shrub/grass/herb communities and eventually to sparse alpine or nival habitats.

Aspect: the same elevation can behave like two different climates

Slope orientation changes incoming solar radiation and therefore surface heating and drying. The sign and magnitude of this
effect depend on latitude and season. In the Northern Hemisphere, for example, south-facing slopes often receive more
direct annual solar input than north-facing slopes, but local cloud, vegetation, slope angle, and atmospheric conditions
matter. Aspect can therefore shift snowmelt timing and soil moisture enough to alter community composition within a single
elevation band.

Evidence: global mountain studies show biodiversity patterns associated with temperature, rainfall, relief, erosion, and soil
heterogeneity, demonstrating that terrain and geological history interact with climate rather than acting as isolated controls.

Reference: Antonelli et al. (2018), Nature Geoscience. https://doi.org/10.1038/s41561-018-0236-z

4. WATER MOVES DOWNHILL - AND BIOMES FOLLOW THE
WATER

Once water reaches the ground, terrain becomes a hydrologic sorting machine. A steep convex slope may shed rainfall
quickly. A hollow may collect runoff and sediment. A floodplain may be inundated repeatedly. A terrace may sit above the
active flood zone and drain more freely. These small changes can determine whether the same regional climate produces
forest, woodland, wet meadow, marsh, shrubland, or grassland.

     Terrain redistributes water: ridge, slope, hollow, terrace, and floodplain can host different plant communities.

                                                         wet valley / floodplain

Upper slopes: faster drainage, thinner soils             Valleys: water + sediment accumulation  Aspect: changes solar exposure and drying

Figure 3. Cross-sectional terrain model. Runoff and subsurface flow concentrate in depressions and valley bottoms. That water movement changes soil
saturation, nutrient transport, and disturbance frequency, producing a fine-scale mosaic of vegetation.

Floodplains are especially important because the terrain-hydrology relationship is time-dependent. A floodplain can shift
among wet and dry states, and its vegetation is shaped by flood depth, duration, frequency, sediment deposition, channel
migration, and groundwater connection. Vegetation in turn modifies roughness, sediment retention, and flow, so terrain and
vegetation can become a feedback system rather than a one-way control.

Reference: Noe (2013), "Interactions among hydrogeomorphology, vegetation, and nutrient biogeochemistry in floodplain ecosystems." DOI:
https://doi.org/10.1016/B978-0-12-374739-6.00338-9

Drainage can create "hidden sub-biomes"

Within a grassland or forest biome, a local depression with persistent saturated soil can support wetland species that would
not survive on an adjacent ridge. Conversely, rocky ridges with thin soil can support drought-adapted shrubs in the middle of
a humid forest region. These are not necessarily separate global biomes; they are local ecological expressions generated by
terrain and water availability.

5. GEOLOGY AND SOIL: THE TERRAIN UNDER THE TERRAIN

Topography determines where material accumulates or is removed, while geology determines what that material is made of.
Weathering turns rock into soil; slope processes move soil downhill; water sorts particles; organic matter accumulates;
plants alter the soil they occupy. The resulting soil controls root access to water and nutrients and can therefore reinforce or
oppose the vegetation pattern expected from climate alone.

A global soil analysis found that climate, parent material, vegetation, and topography together explain much more variation
in ecosystem properties than soil age alone. Another global soil-climate-biome analysis found strong relationships between
soil properties, temperature, precipitation, and biome, reinforcing the idea that soil is part of the climate-biome-terrain
coupling rather than a passive layer beneath plants.

6. TERRAIN EXPRESSION OF BIOMES

Tropical forest

Lowland basins and wet slopes can support tall, layered forests. On steep tropical mountains, elevation, cloud immersion,
and thin/young soils can produce montane forests and cloud forests. Valley bottoms may contain swamp or floodplain
forests, while exposed ridges can be shorter and more wind-stressed.

Savanna and tropical dry systems

Small changes in water table, soil depth, fire frequency, and drainage can change tree cover. Deep, well-drained soils may
carry taller woody vegetation where rainfall permits, while shallow rocky soils or frequently burned areas can remain
grass-dominated.

Temperate forest

Mountain windward slopes can be wetter than nearby lowlands. Valley floors may be frost-prone because cold dense air
pools at night, altering species composition. Limestone, sandstone, volcanic ash, and glacial deposits can each create
different soil conditions within the same climatic forest zone.

Grassland and shrub-steppe

Broad flat terrain favors widespread grasses where water limitation or fire prevents closed forest. Hills and mountains create
a patchwork of shallow soils, snow traps, riparian strips, and sheltered pockets that can support shrubs or trees inside an
otherwise grass-dominated regional biome.

Desert

Deserts are highly structured by landform. Fans, playas, dunes, rocky pavements, canyon floors, and ephemeral stream
channels differ in water storage and disturbance. A single desert can therefore contain many sharply contrasting
"micro-biomes" separated by only a few kilometers.

Boreal and tundra

Flat, cold lowlands can be surprisingly wet because evaporation is low and drainage is impeded by permafrost or shallow
frozen layers. Raised ground, ridges, wind-exposed slopes, and south-facing aspects can be drier and warmer, creating a
mosaic from wet sedge to shrub tundra to open woodland.

 IMPORTANT

 A biome label is a regional summary. Terrain is one reason the same broad biome can contain forests, wetlands,
 shrublands, grasslands, bare rock, and many transitional communities side by side.

10. A REAL-WORLD METHOD: PREDICT THE LOCAL BIOME
FROM TERRAIN

For worldbuilding, simulation, environmental modeling, or simply interpreting a landscape, you can estimate the likely
vegetation pattern by moving through the following sequence.

1. Establish the regional climate

Estimate latitude, seasonal temperature, annual precipitation, precipitation seasonality, prevailing winds, and distance from
ocean.

2. Locate the terrain

Measure elevation, slope, aspect, relief, and position relative to mountain barriers or major water bodies.

3. Predict local moisture

Ask where precipitation is concentrated, where runoff collects, how quickly water drains, and whether snow or groundwater
stores it.

4. Infer the soil environment

Use parent rock, sediment type, erosion/deposition, organic matter, texture, depth, pH, and water table as filters.

5. Add disturbance

Fire, grazing, floods, landslides, storms, avalanches, drought, and human land use can maintain an alternative vegetation
state.

6. Select the vegetation structure

Forest, woodland, shrubland, grassland, wetland, desert scrub, alpine vegetation, or sparse/bare ground.

7. Name the regional unit

Only after understanding the physical setting should you assign a biome/ecoregion/subtype label. The label is the summary,
not the mechanism.

 EXAMPLE

 Suppose two valleys sit at similar latitude and elevation. Valley A is on the windward side of a mountain, has deep volcanic
 soil, frequent cloud, and a perennial stream. Valley B lies in the rain shadow, has shallow rocky soil, and an intermittent
 stream. Even without changing latitude, A could support dense forest while B supports open woodland, shrubland, or
 grassland. Terrain changed the local water and soil regime enough to move the sites into different ecological expressions
 of the same regional climate.

 TAKEAWAY

 A useful causal picture is: climate sets the envelope -> terrain redistributes energy and water -> geology/soil
 controls storage and access -> disturbance selects which vegetation persists -> vegetation feeds back on soil
 and landform -> the resulting recurring pattern is what we call a biome or sub-biome.
