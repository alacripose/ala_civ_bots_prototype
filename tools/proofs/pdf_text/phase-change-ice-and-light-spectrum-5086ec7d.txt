1. What a phase change actually is

A phase change is a change in the collective physical state of matter. For water near everyday pressures, the familiar
phases are solid ice, liquid water, and water vapor. The molecules are still H2O in every case; what changes is their
organization, motion, and thermodynamic state.

Melting and freezing are energy-transfer problems

When ice melts at its melting temperature, added energy does not immediately show up as a temperature increase.
Instead, energy is used to change the molecular arrangement from the solid to the liquid. The amount is the latent heat of
fusion. The reverse process - freezing - releases approximately the same latent heat.

                             For a mass m undergoing a phase change at equilibrium:

                                                                Q = m Lf

For water near 0 C, Lf is about 333.5 kJ/kg. That means freezing 1 kg of water releases about 333.5 kJ of latent heat,
while melting 1 kg of ice requires about the same amount under comparable conditions.

Why temperature can stay near 0 C while ice melts

Suppose you place ice at about 0 C in a warmer environment. Heat can flow into the ice, but the temperature can remain
close to 0 C while the ice is converting to liquid. Once the solid is gone, additional heat raises the liquid-water
temperature. The same logic appears in reverse during freezing: heat must be removed, including the latent heat released
as the liquid becomes solid.

2. Why water makes an unusual ice

Ordinary terrestrial ice at modest pressure is mainly hexagonal ice Ih. Its crystal arrangement is controlled by hydrogen
bonding: each H2O molecule tends to form a directional network that produces an open lattice. This makes ice Ih less
dense than liquid water, which is why ordinary ice floats.

The melting curve slopes the "wrong" way

For most substances, increasing pressure raises the melting temperature. Water is unusual because the solid is less
dense than the liquid. Along the ice-water equilibrium line, increasing pressure slightly lowers the melting temperature.
Thermodynamically this follows from the Clapeyron relation:

                                                           dP/dT = ∆S / ∆V

For melting, ∆S is positive. For ordinary ice Ih melting to liquid water, ∆V = Vliquid - Vice is negative, so the slope dP/dT is
negative. The effect is real but small at ordinary pressures; dramatic claims about everyday pressure "melting ice" are
often overstated.

3. How liquid water becomes ice: nucleation

Cooling below 0 C does not guarantee instantaneous freezing. Liquid water can persist in a supercooled state when there
is no sufficiently favorable site for an ice nucleus to form. Freezing begins when a stable ice embryo appears and grows.

Homogeneous vs. heterogeneous nucleation

• Homogeneous nucleation: an ice embryo forms within the water itself. It generally requires very strong supercooling
for tiny pure droplets.

• Heterogeneous nucleation: a surface or particle - such as mineral dust, biological material, a container wall, or another
ice particle - helps initiate the ice structure. This is much more common in natural environments.

The exact freezing temperature of a droplet depends on droplet size, composition, cooling history, impurities, and the
microscopic properties of the nucleating interface. There is therefore no single temperature at which every bit of water in
nature suddenly freezes. Atmospheric ice-nucleation research treats freezing as a probabilistic process rather than a
perfect switch.

What happens at the moving ice-water boundary

Once a stable crystal nucleus forms, water molecules near the interface become incorporated into the growing solid
lattice. The interface releases latent heat, so the local temperature and heat transport matter. Crystal shape also depends
on temperature, supersaturation, impurities, and growth direction. This is why snow crystals can develop plates, needles,
columns, dendrites, and many intermediate forms.

Useful deeper reading: Koop (2004) reviews homogeneous ice nucleation in water and aqueous solutions, while Ickes et al. (2015) examines the
thermodynamic and kinetic parameters used in classical nucleation theory.

Why structure matters for light

Light interacts with the electric polarization of molecules and the geometry of the material. In a crystal, molecular
vibrations and electronic transitions occur at characteristic frequencies. The optical response is summarized by a complex
refractive index:

                                                         m(λ) = n(λ) + i k(λ)

The real part n controls refraction and phase velocity; the imaginary part k is tied to absorption. For ice,
wavelength-dependent measurements of n and k are the basis for predicting transmission, reflection, scattering, and
absorption.

5. Where "light" sits in the spectrum

Light is electromagnetic radiation. The same physics applies from radio waves through microwaves, infrared, visible light,
ultraviolet, X-rays, and beyond, but the interaction with ice changes because the wavelength changes relative to
molecular and structural length scales.

Gamma / X-ray  UV                          VISIBLE  IR                                Microwave  Radio

               400-700 nm = visible light

                                                    1 um = 1000 nm 10 um = 10,000 nm

Different wavelength, different interaction

• Ultraviolet: stronger electronic absorption appears as wavelength becomes sufficiently short.

• Visible (roughly 0.4-0.7 um): pure ice is a very weak absorber; clean, bubble-free ice can transmit visible light over long
distances.

• Near-infrared (about 0.7-2.5 um): water-molecule vibrational overtones and combinations create pronounced
absorption bands.

• Mid/far infrared: molecular rotations/librations and lattice motions produce strong absorption features; ice becomes
much more thermally emissive/absorptive.

• Microwave/radio: penetration can again become comparatively large, which is why radar can see through substantial
ice and snow.

The exact boundaries between spectral bands vary by convention; the physics is continuous even though we use labels
such as visible, near-IR, and thermal IR.

6. The visible spectrum of ice: why ice can look blue

The visible portion of sunlight spans roughly 400-700 nm. Pure ice is unusually transparent over much of this range, but it
is not perfectly non-absorbing. Its absorption coefficient increases toward the red end of the visible spectrum.

Selective absorption gives long-path ice a blue bias

A useful way to think about transmission through a uniform absorbing material is the Beer-Lambert relation:

                                                     I(x,λ) = I0(λ) exp[-α(λ)x]

Here α(λ) is the wavelength-dependent absorption coefficient and x is the path length through the material. A key result
from measured optical properties is that the absorption length of clean ice is much shorter for red light than for blue light.
Warren (2019) summarizes approximate absorption lengths of about 2 m at 700 nm versus about 200 m at 400 nm for ice
Ih near the melting point.

So a short, clean piece of ice may look essentially colorless. A glacier, thick iceberg, deep crevasse, or very long internal
optical path can selectively suppress more red light, making the surviving light relatively bluer. The effect is not that ice
"contains blue dye"; it follows from the wavelength dependence of absorption combined with the geometry of light paths.

Why snow is white instead of blue

Snow is made of many small ice grains separated by air. A photon can refract and reflect repeatedly at ice-air interfaces.
Those multiple scattering events send light back out of the snow before the photon travels meters through solid ice.
Because visible absorption is weak over the much shorter individual ice paths, the spectrum remains broadly bright and
snow looks white. Grain size and impurities then modify the result.

7. The infrared spectrum is where ice becomes much
more selective

In the infrared, the H2O molecule has vibrational and rotational degrees of freedom that couple strongly to
electromagnetic radiation. Ice therefore develops strong absorption bands that are much more pronounced than its visible
absorption.

Important near-IR and thermal-IR regions

Near-IR: features around roughly 1.5 and 2.0 um are especially useful for distinguishing snow/ice from many other
surfaces in remote sensing. Stronger absorption around ~3 um is associated with water's fundamental vibrational
absorption and is particularly strong.

Thermal IR: ice has strong molecular absorption/emission features in the several-to-tens-of-micrometre range. This
matters because Earth emits thermal radiation primarily in the infrared. Natural snow is therefore highly emissive in much
of the thermal IR; Warren (2019) reports snow emissivity around 98-99%.

Absorption is not the same as reflection

When light encounters ice, the outcome can be a combination of reflection, refraction, transmission, scattering, and
absorption. At a clean air-ice boundary, part of the incoming light reflects immediately because the refractive indices differ.
The transmitted portion changes direction according to Snell's law and may later encounter another boundary. In snow,
the enormous number of ice-air interfaces makes scattering the dominant visible-light effect.

A practical mental model

Think of a photon entering real ice as taking a path through a maze. At every interface it can change direction, reflect, or
transmit; along every piece of its path inside the ice, it has a wavelength-dependent probability of being absorbed. The
final color is determined by the distribution of path lengths plus the material's optical constants.

9. Putting phase change and optics together

The phase change itself can alter how ice interacts with light because freezing reorganizes the material. A liquid-to-solid
transition can create crystals, grain boundaries, bubbles, cracks, inclusions, and changing surface geometry. At the
microscopic level the optical constants are controlled by molecular structure; at the macroscopic level the observed signal
depends on the distribution of interfaces and path lengths.

Example: a freezer ice cube

• As water cools, dissolved gases become less soluble and can form bubbles.

• Freezing begins at nucleation sites and a solidification front moves through the water.

• Impurities and gases may be pushed into the remaining liquid and become concentrated or trapped as the front moves.

• The final cube can therefore contain bubbles, cracks, trapped solute, and different crystal textures.

• Those features scatter visible light strongly, making the cube look white or cloudy even though pure bubble-free ice is
highly transparent.

Example: glacier ice

• Snow grains compact and recrystallize over time, reducing air space.

• Pressure and annealing can eliminate some bubbles and alter grain structure.

• Long optical paths through relatively clean ice can make red absorption accumulate, producing a blue appearance in
deep ice.

• Impurities, fractures, bubbles, and debris modify both scattering and absorption.

Example: satellite remote sensing

Sensors exploit wavelength-dependent reflection and absorption. Visible wavelengths can be sensitive to surface
brightness and impurities; near-IR bands are especially useful for distinguishing snow and ice and for inferring
grain-size-related properties; thermal IR probes emitted radiation; microwave and radar wavelengths can penetrate snow
and ice to depths that visible light cannot.
