How to use this document

This document is a practical companion to the OpenPBR Surface specification. It explains the physics behind the controls
and provides implementation checks for a renderer or material pipeline. It is not a substitute for the authoritative OpenPBR
specification or a promise that every application implements every parameter identically. OpenPBR is a shared surface
shading model maintained as an Academy Software Foundation / MaterialX project; it is not itself a renderer. [1, 2]

Central idea

     Energy conservation is about the whole light budget, not a single highlight.
     For each amount of incident light, the model must account for what is reflected, transmitted or scattered onward, and
     absorbed. The same photon cannot independently fund a full-strength diffuse lobe, a full-strength specular lobe, and
     a full-strength clear coat.

1. What OpenPBR describes

OpenPBR Surface aims to be an artist-usable, cross-application description of common production materials. It combines a
layered surface structure with parameters for metalness, dielectric specular response, diffuse reflection, subsurface
scattering, transmission, coat, fuzz, thin-film iridescence, emission, opacity and thin-walled geometry. The reference model
is based on the same broad family of concepts used in production shaders, but the specification—not a UI label—is the
contract. [1, 2, 10]

2. Light quantities and where energy lives

A rendering system carries radiance along rays and integrates it over directions. Radiance, L, describes energy arriving or
leaving a point per projected area and solid angle; irradiance, E, is incident power per unit area integrated over a
hemisphere. These quantities are not interchangeable with display RGB values after exposure, tone mapping or encoding.
MaterialX specifies radiance as the central transport quantity and treats RGB as a tristimulus representation rather than a
complete spectral distribution. [2]

Quantity           Symbol / units        Useful interpretation
Radiant power      Φ, watts (W)          Total rate of energy flow.
Radiant intensity  I, W sr⁻¹             Power per direction for a point-like source.
Irradiance         E, W m⁻²              Incoming power per surface area.
Radiance           L, W m⁻² sr⁻¹         Directional light quantity transported by most renderers.
BRDF               fᵣ, sr⁻¹              How incident radiance contributes to reflected radiance, per incoming direction
                                         and projected solid angle.

     Figure 2. Conceptual—not measured—energy partitions. Pigment and volume absorption remove energy; metals reflect strongly and
                                         typically have no diffuse substrate; transparent media may both transmit and absorb.

A surface cannot spend the same incoming energy twice

For a passive, non-emissive surface, the incident energy is partitioned among reflection, transmission/scattering that
eventually exits, and absorption. In a full scene, energy can return through many bounces, but each interaction must
remain bounded. A bright pixel by itself does not prove a violation: a mirror can concentrate reflected light into a narrow
angular distribution. Conservation constrains the integral over directions, not each pixel independently.

     Emission is a separate source term.
     A glowing surface can add energy because it is modeled as emitting light. Its emitted radiance does not come from
     the incident-light budget, although the emitted light still participates in transport when it strikes other surfaces.

3. The rendering equation and the scattering
function

At a surface point x, the outgoing radiance in direction ωₒ consists of emitted radiance plus incident radiance reflected into
that direction from all incoming directions. This is the foundational rendering equation introduced to computer graphics in
its modern form by Kajiya and developed in the broader rendering literature. [3]

                Lₒ(x, ωₒ) = Lₑ(x, ωₒ) + ∫H fᵣ(x, ωᵢ, ωₒ) Lᵢ(x, ωᵢ) cos θᵢ dωᵢ

Here H is the incident hemisphere, fᵣ is the BRDF, and cos θᵢ accounts for projected area. The equation is recursive: Lᵢ may
itself come from other surfaces that reflected or emitted light. Path tracing estimates this integral by sampling paths;
rasterization and real-time pipelines approximate it with direct-light evaluation, image-based lighting, prefiltered
environments and other methods.

Diffuse, glossy and specular are about directionality

“Diffuse” means energy is distributed broadly; “specular” is concentrated around ideal reflection or refraction; “glossy” lies
between. These categories describe angular shape rather than energy amount. A rough mirror and a smooth mirror may
reflect similar total energy while producing radically different-looking highlights. [2, 4]

     Implementation consequence
     A shader should not calculate arbitrary “brightness” for each lobe independently and add the values without a
     budget. Lobe evaluation, mixture weights, sampling PDFs and any attenuation between layers must agree with the
     material model.

4. Energy conservation: the integral condition

A common surface-level definition uses the directional albedo (also called directional reflectance): integrate the BRDF over
all incident directions, weighted by the projected solid angle. For a passive reflective lobe, this result must not exceed one
for any fixed outgoing direction. MaterialX makes this condition explicit for physically plausible BSDFs. [1, 2]

                      Eₒ(ωₒ) = ∫H fᵣ(ωᵢ, ωₒ) cos θᵢ dωᵢ ≤ 1

For a material that both reflects and transmits, the analogous whole-surface statement considers the total reflected and
transmitted fractions. Any remaining energy can be absorbed. In a simplified budget, Eᵣ + Eₜ + Eₐ = 1 for a complete
accounting of incident energy; a passive model must not yield Eᵣ + Eₜ > 1. Practical approximations may leave some
energy unreturned, which leads to the separate idea of energy preservation.

Why conservation alone is not enough

A shader that returns zero radiance can be “energy conserving” in the trivial inequality sense, but it is not a useful model
of a white lossless material. Conversely, a material with real absorption should not be brightened artificially to pass a
white-furnace test. The test configuration must describe an idealized lossless material before energy preservation is
expected. [1]

     Do not compare only the highlight pixel.
     Evaluate the integrated outgoing distribution or run a white-furnace test. A narrow lobe can be very bright at its peak
     and still conserve energy; a broad lobe can have a modest peak and carry substantial integrated energy.

5. Fresnel reflection, dielectrics and metals

Fresnel equations describe the angle-dependent division between reflection and transmission at an interface. At normal
incidence, a non-absorbing dielectric interface has reflectance determined by the refractive indices. For air-to-glass with
IOR around 1.5, this is roughly 4%. As the angle approaches grazing, reflectance approaches one. [1, 4, 5]

                                              F₀ = ((n₁ − n₂) / (n₁ + n₂))² ; IOR 1.5 gives F₀ ≈ 0.04

  Figure 3. Exact unpolarized dielectric Fresnel curve for an air-to-IOR-1.5 interface. A value near four percent at normal incidence does not
                                                    mean that only four percent of light can ever be reflected at all angles.

Dielectrics

For common non-metals (plastic, wood, paint, stone, water, glass), specular reflection is largely governed by refractive
index and Fresnel. Light not reflected at the interface may enter the body, where it can scatter, be absorbed, transmit
through, or eventually emerge diffusely. In a layered model, that underlying response must be attenuated by the interface
behavior; otherwise the material gets a full specular lobe and a full diffuse response using the same incoming light.

Conductors / metals

Metals have a complex, wavelength-dependent refractive index. Their Fresnel reflection is strongly colored for materials
such as gold and copper and often high across much of the visible range. They are typically modeled as opaque
conductors, so ordinary diffuse color from a dielectric substrate is not the right physical picture. OpenPBR uses a dedicated
conductor-like base response and an art-directable metallic parametrization. [1, 2]

     OpenPBR controls to know
     specular_ior sets the dielectric IOR; specular_roughness controls the interface microfacet width;
     base_metalness interpolates between dielectric and metal base models; base_color participates differently in
     dielectric base color and conductor Fresnel parametrization. Consult the exact model version before transferring
     settings between tools.

6. Why diffuse and specular must share a
budget

A diffuse term is not a separate reservoir of energy from the specular term. For a dielectric surface, Fresnel-reflected light
never enters the body; only the remaining transmitted portion can feed the diffuse/subsurface response behind the
interface. An implementation can express the principle by scaling the base response by a function related to the energy
not reflected at the interface. OpenPBR’s derivation uses directional-albedo balancing for this purpose. [1]

                                        f_dielectric ≈ f_specular + (1 − E[f_specular]) · f_substrate

This is a conceptual form: E[f_specular] is an integrated directional reflectance, not simply the Fresnel value at the view
angle nor a scalar copied from a specular_weight knob. A production implementation may require angular integration,
compensation, and layer-specific details.

The visible result

At a grazing angle, a dielectric’s Fresnel reflection rises. The base contribution should correspondingly diminish because
less light enters the substrate. In a well-balanced shader the specular highlight does not sit on top of a fully untouched
diffuse layer. This is why the diffuse lobe may look darker around a strong reflection even though the diffuse albedo
texture did not change.

What metallic does to the balance

As the OpenPBR base moves toward metalness, the model transitions toward conductor reflection. The familiar diffuse-like
base contribution is reduced or removed because light is not treated as entering an opaque dielectric body and diffusely
returning. A half-metal setting is artistically useful, but it represents a mixing model—not a universal physical substance.

7. Microfacets and the role of roughness

Microfacet models treat a rough surface as a distribution of tiny, locally specular facets. A common microfacet BRDF
combines a normal-distribution function D, a Fresnel term F, and masking-shadowing factor G. One standard reflection
form is shown below. [2, 4, 5, 7]

                                          fᵣ(ωᵢ, ωₒ) = D(ωₕ) F(ωᵢ, ωₕ) G₂(ωᵢ, ωₒ) / (4 cos θᵢ cos θₒ)

The half-vector ωₕ is derived from the incident and outgoing directions. D describes how likely microfacet normals are
oriented; F determines the Fresnel response at each microfacet; G accounts for microfacets hidden from the incident or
outgoing direction. OpenPBR / MaterialX uses a GGX-family microfacet description for its common dielectric and conductor
specular interfaces. [1, 2]

  Figure 4. Illustration only: the same normalized energy distribution can be narrow or broad. A shader whose roughness changes the lobe
width should avoid inadvertently changing total reflectance, except where the model’s intended physical behavior or approximations specify

                                                                                                   otherwise.

Roughness is not a brightness slider

For an ideal energy-normalized model, increasing roughness broadens the reflection while broadly preserving its total
integrated energy. The peak drops because that energy is spread across more directions. In a finite renderer, measured
brightness can still change due to sampling variance, environment-map filtering, limited path depth, denoising, clamping
or missing multiple-scattering energy.

Geometry and normal maps

The masking-shadowing term matters: facets can hide one another. Incorrect geometric attenuation can produce bad
grazing highlights and incorrect normalization. Heitz’s analysis derives constraints on masking functions from visible
microsurface area and explains why choosing G is tied to a microsurface assumption. Normal maps are not automatically a
license to ignore geometric occlusion or change material energy accounting. [7]

     Test across roughness, not at just one value.
     Sweep specular_roughness from near zero to very rough while holding the environment and all other parameters
     fixed. Look for changes in integrated output, not merely the highlight shape.

8. Missing energy in single-scattering
microfacet models

A common implementation evaluates the main specular event as if the light reflects once from a microfacet and then
leaves the surface. Real microsurfaces can have multiple internal bounces: one facet may reflect light toward another
facet, and that second event may send energy toward the camera. A single-scattering microfacet BRDF omits those paths.
The omission becomes more visible for rough surfaces because occlusion and facet-to-facet paths are more important. [2,
6]

What energy compensation is trying to fix

Multiple-scattering compensation adds the energy that the single-scatter model omitted, while keeping the overall BSDF
normalized. Different techniques exist: explicit multi-scatter models, analytical or tabulated corrections, and
approximations based on single-scatter directional albedo. One correction family is documented in the MaterialX PBS
specification as a multiplier of the single-scatter BRDF; it should be understood as one implementation strategy rather than
a universal OpenPBR equation. [2, 6]

                                          fᵣᶜᵒᵐᵖ ≈ fᵣ · (1 + F̄ · (1 − Eₛₛ) / Eₛₛ)

In this schematic form, Eₛₛ denotes a directional albedo for a unit-Fresnel single-scattering microfacet model and F̄
represents a suitable averaged Fresnel factor. The exact normalization, edge cases, sampling, and implementation details
matter; do not paste this shorthand into a shader without following the source specification.

9. Layering: coat, fuzz and substrate

OpenPBR models materials as a stack of interacting structures. If an upper layer reflects some light, the underlying layer
must receive less. If the upper layer absorbs light, that absorbed portion does not reach the base. If it transmits light, the
transmitted part is attenuated on its way through. A naive sum of independent lobes ignores this path budget. [1, 2]

           Figure 5. Conceptual layer stack. Real implementations need to account for direction, Fresnel reflectance, absorption and the
                                                                    approximations used to represent multiple passes.

A useful layering abstraction

                                                                 f_layered ≈ f_top + T_top · f_base

Here T_top represents the fraction made available to the lower layer under the model’s directional approximation.
MaterialX defines a vertical-layering transmittance for BSDFs and composes the transmittance of nested layers; for
common interface lobes the pass-through is related to one minus reflected directional albedo, with absorption treated
separately. This is more disciplined than adding full top and base BSDFs. [2]

Coat

A clear coat adds another dielectric interface with its own IOR, roughness, coverage/weight and color/absorption behavior.
Adding coat weight must not increase the full-strength energy available to both the coat and base. The coat’s Fresnel
response and attenuation change the base that remains visible. A physically plausible car-paint appearance depends on
this relation, not simply on drawing a second highlight over metallic paint.

Fuzz

Fuzz represents a fiber-like scattering layer. It also changes the light available to the structure beneath it. Treat fuzz as a
model component with its own directional response and attenuation, not merely an emissive rim term. If a custom fur/fuzz
effect intentionally violates the model, document that departure.

     Watch for double attenuation as well as missing attenuation.
     If the top lobe is attenuated once inside the OpenPBR BSDF and again by a renderer-specific layer node, the base
     may become too dark. One layer stack should have one well-defined energy bookkeeping convention.

10. Transmission, absorption and subsurface
transport

For a dielectric, reflected energy and transmitted energy are linked by the interface’s Fresnel behavior. After transmission
into a body, light may be absorbed, scattered, refracted, transmitted out of the far side, or emerge from a different
location. A surface BRDF alone cannot describe all of these paths. OpenPBR exposes distinct glossy-diffuse, subsurface and
translucent-base configurations to cover different appearance regimes. [1, 2, 5]

Beer-Lambert attenuation

A homogeneous absorbing medium attenuates light exponentially with path length. OpenPBR’s artist-facing transmission
color/depth pair is one way to derive an extinction coefficient per color channel when transmission depth is positive. The
color indicates the surviving fraction at the specified distance, not a free bonus tint independent of physical path length.
[1]

                                                       T(d) = exp(−σₜ d) and σₜ = −ln(T_ref) / d_ref

T(d) is transmittance over distance d; σₜ is extinction (per-channel in an RGB approximation); T_ref is the transmission
color at reference distance d_ref. Scattering can redirect the surviving energy; absorption removes energy from the light
field. Extinction combines both effects along the original ray.

Subsurface is not the same as surface diffuse

Diffuse reflection is an efficient approximation for light that enters and exits near a surface point after many unresolved
scattering events. Subsurface scattering carries spatial displacement and material-scale dependence. The color and
mean-free-path controls affect appearance, but conservation still requires the amount of energy that emerges to be
compatible with energy entering and leaving the object.

Thin-walled materials

Leaves, paper and thin films may be better represented with a thin-walled model than a thick closed volume. It changes
how transmission and internal travel are approximated. Do not use the thin-walled option just to remove inconvenient
refraction artifacts on a physically thick object unless the visual compromise is intended.

     Keep units and scale coherent.
     A transmission_depth of 1 means a distance in the scene’s declared length units. If one asset uses centimeters and
     another interprets the same numeric depth as meters, the color attenuation will be wildly different. Document units
     and conversions in the pipeline.

11. The white-furnace test

A white furnace is a uniform white environment that illuminates the test object equally from every direction. For idealized
lossless white material configurations, the object should eventually merge into the environment when light transport is
solved with sufficient path depth. OpenPBR’s specification explicitly describes this as a test of energy preservation and lists
configurations involving white dielectric and metallic bases, coat, fuzz, thin films, roughness and anisotropy. [1]

Figure 6. Conceptual white-furnace arrangement. A visually plausible single frame is not enough; the test should use a stable, uniform
                                                           environment and enough bounces for the target model.

A reproducible test protocol

Step               Procedure

1. Environment     Use a uniform white environment with a known linear radiance or unit intensity. Disable contrasty HDRI maps.

2. Geometry        Use a closed sphere or another well-behaved primitive. Keep normals and geometry fixed during tests.

3. Ideal material  Start with white, lossless values and turn off emission. Use an OpenPBR configuration named in the current
                   white-furnace section.

4. Transport       Use enough bounces / converged samples. Fix exposure, view transform, denoiser and clamping.

5. Sweep           Test low-to-high specular and coat roughness; then add coat, fuzz, thin film, anisotropy and supported normal maps.

6. Measure         Compare linear render values or integrated energy against the environment, not only tone-mapped screenshots.

7. Repeat          Run multiple seeds and renderer backends; record software version and material parameters.

Interpretation

If the all-white lossless sphere appears too dark, investigate lost energy, insufficient bounces, transmission accounting,
layer attenuation, the environment prefilter and denoising. If it is too bright or exceeds the environment, investigate
duplicated lobes, double-adding reflection, incorrect layer weights and normalization. A pigmented or absorbing real
material is not expected to vanish into the white environment; it should return less energy.

13. Practical renderer integration checklist

Material-evaluation layer

□ Choose and pin an OpenPBR specification version. □ Keep shader parameter names and units consistent with the
specification. □ Implement color management at the input/output boundaries, not as hidden BRDF math. □ Separate
surface reflection/transmission (BSDF), subsurface/volume transport (BSSRDF/VDF) and emission (EDF). □ Keep direct
lighting, environment lighting and path tracing on compatible scattering definitions.

Transport and sampling

□ A sample’s throughput weight must match the BSDF value, cosine / projection measure and PDF used to generate the
sample. □ Include transmission when evaluating the whole energy budget. □ Use a layer transmission convention shared
by each layer node. □ If a multiple-scattering correction exists, align evaluation, sampling and PDF accounting. □ Record
path depth and termination thresholds for tests.

Texture and coordinate conventions

□ Define linear working color space and channel interpretation. □ Confirm roughness remapping end-to-end. □ State
whether texture values are perceptual roughness or microfacet alpha. □ Validate tangent frames / anisotropy axes. □ Test
geometric normals and shading normals. □ Specify scene units for subsurface radius and transmission depth.

16. Limits: physical principles versus a
production model

Energy conservation is necessary, not sufficient

A material can conserve energy and still be the wrong material. Energy conservation alone does not guarantee the correct
Fresnel curve, wavelength-dependent metal color, subsurface profile, refraction, anisotropy, polarization behavior or
thin-film interference. It also does not define the artistic meaning of every control. Physically based models are
approximations with chosen degrees of freedom. [1, 2, 4, 8]

RGB is a rendering approximation

Most production pipelines transport linear RGB rather than wavelength-resolved spectral radiance. RGB Fresnel and
absorption colors can match many appearances, but they cannot represent every spectral effect exactly. Dispersion,
narrow spectral interactions, fluorescence and unusual thin-film behavior may require a spectral renderer or carefully
chosen approximations. OpenPBR’s controls should be interpreted within the renderer’s supported transport model. [2]

Reciprocity and transport conventions

Reciprocity means that reversing the light path preserves the material response under the appropriate conventions. It is a
key property of passive ordinary materials and useful for detecting inconsistent BSDFs. Transmission needs particular care
because radiance measures on the two sides of a refractive boundary involve refractive-index factors. A test that swaps
direction vectors without respecting the renderer’s convention may falsely diagnose an error.

Art direction is not banned

An open material model can include tint, weight and other controls that are deliberately art-directable. The engineering
requirement is that the implementation define their meaning and preserve the intended bounds where energy
conservation is promised. If a project intentionally uses nonphysical values to reach a stylized look, flag that material as an
intentional exception rather than silently treating it as a physically faithful reference.

Versioning matters

The OpenPBR website and MaterialX library are living documents. Current MaterialX specification material documents the
OpenPBR Surface Shader Definition v1.1.1, while applications may advertise support for earlier revisions or a subset.
Before comparing results, pin the specification and renderer version and record unsupported parameters. A shared model
name alone is not enough. [1, 2, 10, 11]

     The practical standard
     A trustworthy OpenPBR implementation should explain its energy accounting, produce stable material behavior
     under changing illumination, and come with tests that reveal where it approximates or omits the standard.

17. Compact glossary

Term                         Definition
Albedo / directional albedo  Fraction of incident energy reflected by a scattering function under a defined directional integral; depends on
                             convention and configuration.
Absorption                   Conversion of optical energy to internal energy; removed from the rendered light field.
BSDF                         Bidirectional scattering distribution function; umbrella for reflection and transmission.
BRDF / BTDF                  Reflectance / transmittance distributions for light scattered from a surface.
BSSRDF                       Subsurface distribution connecting incoming and outgoing light at potentially different surface points.
Dielectric                   Non-conducting optical material such as glass, water or plastic; interface reflection is governed by refractive
                             index / Fresnel.
Conductor                    Conductive material, typically a metal, modeled with complex-IOR / conductor Fresnel behavior.
Fresnel                      Angle- and polarization-dependent reflection/transmission behavior at an interface.
IOR (index of refraction)    Dimensionless ratio that governs phase propagation and refraction; it affects dielectric Fresnel reflection.
Microfacet                   A statistical model of rough surfaces as many tiny idealized facets.
NDF / NDF roughness          Normal distribution function describing microfacet orientations; roughness controls its width / shape.
Masking-shadowing G          Geometric attenuation term accounting for microfacets hidden along incident or outgoing directions.
Multiple scattering          Two or more scattering events within a rough microsurface or volume before light exits.
Energy compensation          A correction or model extension that restores physically appropriate energy omitted by a simplified scattering
                             approximation.
White furnace                Uniform white environment test for energy preservation in designated lossless material configurations.
OpenPBR Surface              A standardized, artist-facing material model, maintained as part of the ASWF / MaterialX ecosystem.
Beer-Lambert law             Exponential attenuation of a beam along path length in a medium.
Emission / EDF               Light emitted by a material, treated separately from reflected incident illumination.
Tone mapping                 A display transform that maps scene-linear radiance to a display range; it can obscure energy bugs if used for
                             diagnosis.

Five principles to remember

1. Conserve the whole incident-light budget. 2. Couple diffuse and specular response through Fresnel / layer transmission.
3. Roughness changes distribution and can reveal missing microfacet multiple scattering. 4. Layering attenuates the base;
it is not unweighted addition. 5. Validate with white-furnace and controlled linear-output tests before art-directing
materials.
