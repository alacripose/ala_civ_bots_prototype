Aerosol Optics, Sky Models, LWC & Twilight — Technical Reference

    Aerosol Optical Properties, Sky Radiance Models,
         Cloud LWC Profiles & Twilight Illuminance

                Technical reference for coupling turbidity, visibility, Preetham sky domain,
                         adiabatic liquid-water content, and civil-twilight ambient light

This document addresses four gaps that commonly appear when an analytic clear-sky model (Preetham / Perez)
is extended toward cloudy or low-Sun conditions: (1) the mapping from aerosol optical depth and visibility to the
single turbidity parameter used by Preetham; (2) the validity domain of that sky model and the conceptual
separation of aerosol extinction from cloud scattering; (3) adiabatic liquid-water content (LWC) and liquid-water
path (LWP) profiles that can replace purely declared wet-turbidity gains; and (4) quantitative illuminance curves
through civil twilight for open-horizon ambient lighting.

1. Aerosol Optical Properties and Turbidity

1.1 Aerosol Optical Depth (AOD / AOT)

Aerosol optical depth τa(λ) is the vertical integral of the aerosol extinction coefficient σext(z,λ) from the surface to
the top of the atmosphere:

                                                             τa(λ) = ∫ ∞ 0 σext(z,λ) dz
It is dimensionless and wavelength-dependent. Typical mid-visible (550 nm) values range from <0.05 (pristine
maritime / polar) through 0.1–0.3 (clean continental) to >0.5–1.0 (urban, biomass-burning or dust outbreaks).
AERONET sun-photometer networks report AOD at several discrete wavelengths; satellite products (MODIS,
VIIRS) provide global maps at 550 nm.

1.2 Ångström Exponent

Ångström (1929) observed that aerosol optical thickness follows a power law over the visible–near-IR:
                                                                  τa(λ) = β · λ−α

where β is the Ångström turbidity coefficient (AOD extrapolated to 1 µm) and α is the Ångström exponent. From
two wavelengths:

                                                         α = − ln(τλ1 / τλ2) / ln(λ1 / λ2)
α is inversely related to particle size: fine-mode combustion aerosols give α ≈ 1.5–2.0; coarse dust or sea-salt
give α ≈ 0–0.5; cloud droplets (very large) give α ≈ 0 (hence clouds appear white/grey). Typical continental
averages lie near α ≈ 1.3. Because the power-law is only approximate, α itself varies with wavelength
(“curvature”); multi-wavelength fits or second-order spectral derivatives improve size-mode separation.

1.3 Preetham / Linke Turbidity Factor T

The Preetham (1999) analytic sky model parameterises the atmosphere by a single scalar T (turbidity).
Conceptually T is the ratio of the optical thickness of a hazy atmosphere to that of a clean Rayleigh atmosphere at
the same air mass:

                                                      T = (τRayleigh + τaerosol) / τRayleigh
A perfectly clean atmosphere has T = 1; observed “exceptionally clear” values are ≈1.25, “very clear” ≈1.6, typical
rural 2–3, urban/hazy 4–6, and heavy haze T ≥ 10. Preetham’s analytic fit was performed on data for T ∈ [2, 6];
outside that interval the model is numerically and physically unreliable (see §2).

 A practical conversion from mid-visible AOD (τ550) often used in graphics and solar-energy literature is
 approximately:

                        T ≈ 1 + 16 · τ550 (order-of-magnitude; see also Bodhaine 1999, Kasten–Young)
 More accurate mappings incorporate the Ångström exponent and the relative optical air mass. The Linke turbidity
 factor TL is a closely related quantity used in solar radiation models; linear regressions between TL and Ångström
 β exist for different climate regimes.

 1.4 Visibility / Meteorological Optical Range

 Koschmieder’s law relates daytime horizontal visibility V (meteorological optical range) to the horizontal extinction
 coefficient σext:

                                                      V = (1 / σext) · ln(1 / ε) ≈ 3.912 / σext
 where the contrast threshold ε is conventionally taken as 0.02 (sometimes 0.05). If an exponential scale height H
 for aerosol extinction is assumed, the column AOD can be recovered from surface visibility:

                                                          τa ≈ (3.912 / V) · H − τRayleigh
 Typical aerosol scale heights are 1–2 km. Visibility data are widely archived by weather services and can
 therefore serve as a proxy for historical AOD, although water vapour, fog and precipitation must be filtered. This
 relationship is the physical basis for any “visibility → turbidity” conversion used in a renderer.

 1.5 Scattering vs Extinction; Coupling to Wet Turbidity

 Extinction is the sum of scattering and absorption: σext = σsca + σabs. For most continental aerosols the
 single-scattering albedo ω = σsca/σext lies between 0.85 and 0.95 in the visible; strongly absorbing smoke or
 black-carbon plumes can drop ω below 0.7. Cloud droplets are essentially pure scatterers (ω ≈ 1) with Ångström
 exponent near zero.

 When a renderer introduces a wet-turbidity gain (e.g. WET_TURBIDITY_GAIN scaled by a reference water path),
 the gain is effectively modulating an aerosol-like extinction term. Because cloud optical depth is orders of
 magnitude larger than typical AOD and has a different spectral dependence, the physically consistent approach is
 to keep aerosol turbidity and cloud optical depth as separate quantities and to composite them only at the
 radiance level (see §2.3).

 2. Sky Radiance Models — Validity Domain of Preetham

 2.1 What Preetham Models

 Preetham, Shirley & Smits (SIGGRAPH 1999) fitted an analytic Perez-type luminance distribution to a large set of
 path-traced clear-sky radiances generated by the Nishita et al. (1996) single-scattering model. Inputs are solar
 zenith angle θs, view zenith/azimuth, and turbidity T. The model supplies both relative luminance distribution and
 absolute zenith luminance / chromaticity, making it convenient for real-time graphics. Related supporting formulae
 (Bodhaine 1999 Rayleigh optical depth, Kasten–Young air-mass) are commonly cited alongside the
 implementation.

 2.2 Known Limitations and Numerical Domain

 Independent critical reviews (Zotti, Wilkie & Purgathofer 2007; later quantitative comparisons) establish the
 following practical bounds:

      • Turbidity range. The underlying fit used T ∈ [2, 6]. For T s 1.9 the model produces an unrealistically bright horizon
      glow and can yield negative zenith luminance. For T s 10 the formulae become numerically unstable and the
      physical premise of a “clear-sky” atmosphere ceases to hold.
      • Solar elevation. Results near sunrise/sunset (θs s 85°) are problematic, especially at high turbidity; absolute
      luminances can become negative or diverge.

      • Horizon and colour. Even inside the nominal range the horizon is often too bright and too pink compared with CIE
      Standard General Sky measurements and real clear-sky observations.
      • Ground-only observer. The analytic functions and the accompanying aerial-perspective term assume a
      ground-level observer and a flat-Earth geometry.
      • No clouds. The model contains no cloud optical depth, no multiple scattering inside cloud layers, and no
      broken-cloud geometry. It is strictly a clear-sky (or at most thin-haze) model.
 Consequently, any production renderer that drives Preetham with a turbidity that has been artificially inflated to
 simulate clouds is operating outside the model’s validity domain. The visual result may be plausible for light haze,
 but it is not a physically justified representation of cloud scattering.

 2.3 Clouds Are Not Aerosol — Recommended Separation

 Cloud droplets are typically 5–20 µm in radius — two orders of magnitude larger than accumulation-mode aerosol.
 Their scattering is essentially wavelength-independent (α ≈ 0), their single-scattering albedo is ≈1, and a cloud of
 only a few hundred metres optical depth already reaches τ s 10. Treating cloud water as an additive term inside
 the Preetham turbidity parameter therefore:

      • violates the spectral assumptions of the Ångström / Preetham formulation;
      • pushes T far outside the [2, 6] fit range;
      • cannot reproduce the sharp spatial and angular structure of real cloud fields;
      • cannot capture the transition from single-scattering haze to multiple-scattering white cloud.
 A cleaner architecture keeps:

      1. Clear-sky radiance — Preetham (or the improved Hosek–Wilkie / Hosek–Wilkie spectral models) driven by a true
      aerosol turbidity T derived from AOD or visibility;
      2. Cloud optical properties — separately computed from liquid-water content profiles (or a microphysical
      parameterisation) and composited via a multiple-scattering approximation, a precomputed cloud lookup, or a
      path-traced cloud layer;
      3. Aerial perspective / extinction — applied consistently to both the clear sky and the cloud-scattered light using the
      same aerosol optical depth.
     Practical note: if a legacy “wet turbidity gain” must be retained for artistic control, document it explicitly as a stylisation that
     leaves the physical clear-sky path untouched, and clamp the effective T to the interval [1.8, 8] to avoid numerical
     breakdown.

 3. Adiabatic Liquid-Water Content and Liquid-Water Path

 3.1 Adiabatic Condensation

 In a closed air parcel rising from cloud base without entrainment, water vapour condenses at a rate set by the
 difference between the dry and saturated adiabatic lapse rates. The specific liquid-water mass ql satisfies

                                                             dql = (cp / Lv) (Γd − Γs) dz
 where Γd = g/cp is the dry adiabatic lapse rate and Γs is the saturated moist-adiabatic lapse rate. For shallow
 clouds (depth s 500 m) the condensation rate cq is nearly constant and equal to its value at cloud base, yielding
 the linear profile

                                                           ql(z) ≈ cq(T0, p0) · (z − zbase)

 3.2 Liquid-Water Content (LWC)

 Liquid-water content is the mass of liquid water per unit volume:

                                                                LWC(z) = ρ(z) · ql(z)
 Under the constant-rate approximation this is also linear in height above base:

                                            LWC(z) ≈ cLWC · (z − zbase), cLWC = ρ0 cq(T0, p0)

Typical adiabatic gradients at mid-latitude temperatures are of order 1–2 g m−3 km−1 (i.e. 0.001–0.002 g m−3 m−1).
A 300 m deep adiabatic cloud therefore reaches peak LWC ≈ 0.3–0.6 g m−3 near cloud top.

3.3 Liquid-Water Path (LWP)

The liquid-water path is the vertical integral of LWC:
                                                        LWP = ∫ z_top z_base LWC(z) dz

For a linear (adiabatic) profile LWP = ½ · LWCtop · ∆z. Observationally, microwave radiometers measure LWP
directly; adiabatic LWP computed from cloud-base temperature/pressure and cloud depth usually overestimates
the measured value because real clouds are sub-adiabatic.

3.4 Sub-adiabaticity

Entrainment of dry air, precipitation and turbulent mixing reduce the liquid-water content below the pure adiabatic
value. The adiabaticity factor

                                                       fad = LWPobserved / LWPadiabatic

is typically 0.5–0.9 for shallow stratocumulus and can fall to 0.2–0.5 for deeper or heavily precipitating clouds.
Cloud-net and aircraft studies show that clouds thinner than ≈200 m are often close to adiabatic, while thicker
layers become progressively more sub-adiabatic. A simple practical model is therefore:

                                                      LWC(z) = fad · cLWC · (z − zbase)
with fad prescribed from cloud depth or left as a tunable parameter. This supplies a physically sourced water-path
reference that can replace a purely declared WATER_PATH_REFERENCE_M in a wet-turbidity or
cloud-optical-depth ramp.

4. Twilight / Civil-Twilight Illuminance

4.1 Definitions

Twilight is conventionally divided by solar elevation angle hs (centre of the solar disk, refraction-corrected):

    • Civil twilight: 0° ≥ hs ≥ −6°. Terrestrial objects remain distinguishable; outdoor activities possible without artificial
    light.

    • Nautical twilight: −6° ≥ hs ≥ −12°. Horizon still visible for navigation; most stars appear.
    • Astronomical twilight: −12° ≥ hs ≥ −18°. Sky still measurably brighter than pure night; after −18° the contribution
    of sunlight is negligible.

4.2 Illuminance versus Solar Elevation

4.3 Analytic Approximations for the Ambient Step

For real-time or offline lighting the rapid change near the horizon can be captured by a smooth function of solar
elevation. A convenient practical form used in many graphics and illumination models is a logistic or exponential
decay fitted to the civil-twilight interval:

                                                  E(h) = E0 · 10k·h (h in degrees, h ≤ 0)
with E0 ≈ 400–600 lx at h = 0° and k ≈ 0.4–0.5 per degree through civil twilight, then a shallower slope through
nautical and astronomical twilight. More accurate tabulated or spline representations (e.g. those derived from the
“moonlit” / SQM-twilight data sets) can be pre-computed once and evaluated by lookup. Because the ambient step
at the open horizon is dominated by the clear-sky scattering path, the same turbidity that drives the Preetham
daytime sky should also modulate the absolute scale of the twilight curve; higher turbidity lengthens the twilight
and raises the residual illuminance at a given negative elevation.

When clouds are present the twilight curve is further modified: a broken cloud field can both obscure the last direct
rays and reflect residual sunlight, producing a more abrupt or a more prolonged transition depending on cloud
cover and solar azimuth relative to the observer. A full treatment again requires separating the clear-sky
illuminance from the cloud-scattered contribution.

5. Summary Recommendations for Implementation

    1. Turbidity source. Derive T from measured or climatological AOD (or from visibility via Koschmieder + scale
    height). Keep T inside [1.8, 8] for Preetham; outside that range switch to a different sky model (Hosek–Wilkie, CIE
    Standard General Sky, or a path-traced atmosphere).
    2. Do not fold cloud water into T. Cloud optical depth belongs in a separate cloud-radiance term. A wet-turbidity
    gain is a stylisation; if retained, document and clamp it.
    3. LWC / LWP. Prefer an adiabatic (or sub-adiabatic) profile driven by cloud-base temperature/pressure and
    geometric thickness. The resulting LWP can directly feed a cloud optical-depth parameterisation, replacing a
    hard-coded WATER_PATH_REFERENCE_M.
    4. Twilight ambient. Drive the open-horizon ambient step with a tabulated or analytic illuminance-versus-elevation
    curve that is continuous with the daytime Preetham zenith luminance at h ≈ 0°. Scale the curve by the same turbidity
    used for the daytime sky.
    5. Spectral consistency. Aerosol extinction carries an Ångström exponent; cloud extinction does not. Any
    wavelength-dependent path (spectral rendering, aerial perspective) must treat the two components separately.
