A physical guide to near-surface clouds and water-atmosphere exchange

This guide connects the thermodynamics of fog to the fluid mechanics of spray and foam. It explains why fog forms, why it
can cling to the ground or water, how wind and mixing disperse it, and how breaking waves can turn liquid water into airborne
aerosol particles. Emphasis is placed on mechanism rather than visual label alone.

  Core distinction: fog is a suspension of tiny liquid droplets (or, in ice fog, ice particles) in air. Foam is a collection of gas
  bubbles enclosed by liquid films. Spray is liquid droplets launched into air. These can interact, but they are not the same physical
  state.

Includes ground fog, lake and sea fog, steam fog, cold-water advection fog, sea foam and bubble-burst aerosols, waterfalls and surf mist, visibility
physics, dissipation, field diagnosis, a compact modeling framework, and research links.

1. The system at a glance

Visible fog and mist are outcomes of two related but different pathways: phase change (water vapor condenses into droplets)
and mechanical atomization (liquid water is broken into airborne droplets). A third pathway, bubble bursting, couples foam
to aerosol generation. All three are shaped by humidity, temperature, turbulence, and droplet-size distributions. [1-5]

2. How invisible vapor becomes visible fog

Air contains water vapor mixed with nitrogen, oxygen, and other gases. The vapor pressure at saturation rises strongly with
temperature: warm air can coexist with more vapor before condensation begins than cold air can. Fog develops when
near-surface air becomes saturated and droplets can form and grow on suitable aerosol particles. Cooling is common, but
adding vapor can also bring air to saturation. [1,2,6]

2.1 Relative humidity and dew point

Relative humidity (RH) is the ratio of actual vapor pressure to saturation vapor pressure at the current temperature. Dew point
is the temperature to which the air must cool, at roughly constant pressure and vapor content, to reach saturation. As air
temperature approaches dew point, the saturation deficit shrinks. At saturation, condensation is favored if droplets can
nucleate or existing droplets can grow.

  Field reading: if air temperature is only 0-2 C above dew point, fog may be close, but it is not guaranteed. Wind, vertical mixing,
  aerosols, surface temperature, and whether the air is actually cooling or moistening determine the outcome.

2.2 Why aerosols matter

Water often condenses on pre-existing particles rather than appearing spontaneously in perfectly clean air. Hygroscopic
particles such as sea salt can take up water as humidity rises. Once activated, a cloud or fog droplet grows by vapor diffusion
and latent-heat exchange. The number and chemistry of available nuclei influence droplet number, average size, optical
extinction, and the speed at which water is redistributed among droplets. [4,7,8]

3. Ground fog, radiation fog, and valley fog

 Ground fog is a practical description for fog concentrated in a shallow layer close to the surface. It is often associated with
 nocturnal radiative cooling, but the label does not uniquely identify one process. Radiation fog develops when the ground
 loses longwave energy, cools the adjacent air, and brings it to saturation. It is favored by moist near-surface air, clear or
 mostly clear skies, light winds, and sufficient time for cooling. [2,3]

 3.1 A typical overnight sequence

 • Evening: the surface begins losing stored heat. Moist soil, wetlands, irrigation, lakes, and vegetation can keep the
 near-surface layer humid.
 • Late night: the surface and adjacent air cool. If temperature reaches dew point, droplets form around nuclei. A light breeze
 may help mix moisture into a shallow layer; stronger wind can mix in warmer or drier air and prevent a dense layer.
 • Predawn: continued cooling can increase liquid water content and deepen fog. In valleys, cold-air drainage may reinforce
 pooling; a temperature inversion can cap vertical mixing.
 • After sunrise: surface heating raises air temperature and saturation vapor pressure. Droplets evaporate if the air becomes
 subsaturated, though thick fog can persist where sunlight is weak or moisture supply remains strong.

 3.2 Why fog likes low places

 Cold air is denser than warmer air at similar pressure and tends to flow downslope under gravity. Valleys can therefore
 accumulate a humid, cool layer while ridges remain clear. Topography also reduces ventilation and can shelter the surface
 from stronger winds. Yet not every valley fog is purely radiative: moisture advection, evaporation, precipitation, and slope
 lifting may contribute. [2,3]

 3.3 Dew, frost, fog, and wet vegetation are different

 Dew is liquid water deposited directly onto a surface when that surface cools below the local dew point. Frost forms as ice
 deposition or freezing under appropriate conditions. Fog consists of airborne droplets or ice particles. A foggy landscape can
 collect additional liquid by interception on leaves, needles, fences, and buildings, but that surface water is not itself airborne
 fog.

   Ground-fog diagnostic: inspect vertical depth, wind, cloud cover, surface wetness, valley position, and the
   temperature-dew-point spread. A thin fog layer that leaves treetops clear suggests a shallow near-surface process; fog attached
   to a slope may instead indicate upslope flow or advection.

4. Fog in relation to lakes, rivers, and oceans

A water body is not simply a source of “more humidity.” Its surface temperature relative to the air, the stability of the lowest
atmosphere, wind direction, and air-water exchange jointly determine whether fog forms. Lake and sea fog can arise when
the water cools the air above it; steam fog can arise when warmer water adds vapor to colder air. [2,5,6]

4.1 The key temperature difference

For advection fog, compare the air dew point with the surface temperature of the water. If moist air is advected over water
cooler than its dew point, the air can be cooled to saturation. For steam fog, compare the water temperature with the air
temperature: evaporation from warmer water adds vapor to a cold layer, which can rapidly reach saturation. This is not a strict
binary in nature; fog layers can evolve through simultaneous cooling, evaporation, turbulent mixing, and advection.

4.2 Why big lakes behave differently from small ponds

Large lakes and oceans have substantial heat capacity, so their temperatures change more slowly than land surface
temperatures. This seasonal lag creates strong air-water contrasts during transitions: cold water can undercut warm moist air
in spring, while water that retains summer heat can send vapor into cold autumn air. Small, shallow ponds respond more
quickly to weather and can cool or warm faster. Local wind, fetch, shoreline geometry, and cold-air drainage determine how
far a fog bank extends.

 4.3 Bodies of water do not always make fog

 If the air is too dry, there is insufficient vapor for saturation. Strong mixing can dilute a near-surface moist layer. If water and
 air temperatures are similar, neither cooling nor evaporative moistening may be strong enough. A visible spray plume may still
 form from breaking waves or splash, but that is mechanical droplet production rather than a condensation fog.

5. Foam, whitecaps, bubble bursting, and sea spray

 Foam is a gas-in-liquid structure: many bubbles are separated by thin liquid films. Surfactants, including natural organic
 material from plankton and decaying biomass, can lower surface tension and slow drainage or rupture of these films. Foam is
 often transient; its lifetime depends on bubble-size distribution, film thickness, liquid viscosity, surfactant chemistry, and the
 rate at which waves create and destroy bubbles. [8-10]

 5.1 What happens when a bubble bursts?

 • Bubble formation: breaking waves, waterfalls, turbulence, or splashing drive air into water. Dissolved gases and
 surfactants influence bubble behavior.
 • Bubble rise: buoyancy carries bubbles toward the surface; small bubbles can be transported or trapped by turbulent water.
 • Film-cap rupture: the thin bubble cap breaks into fragments, producing film drops. The exact number and size vary
 strongly with bubble size and surface chemistry.
 • Cavity collapse and jetting: after the cap breaks, the cavity recoils and can eject a jet that pinches into additional drops.
 • Atmospheric fate: larger drops may fall back quickly; smaller droplets can be transported by turbulence, evaporate, or
 retain water and grow if ambient humidity is high.

 5.2 Why sea foam can be white or brown

 A dense foam patch scatters visible light repeatedly at many gas-liquid interfaces, producing a pale or white appearance even
 though a thin film of water is transparent. Brown or tan sea foam may contain concentrated dissolved or suspended organic
 material, algal products, or sediment. Color alone does not establish toxicity or biological origin. The foam patch is a
 liquid-surface feature; the droplets it emits are a distinct airborne population. [9,10]

 5.3 Spray versus humid air

 Windy surf can look like fog even when the air is not saturated. Mechanical spray consists of droplets launched from waves
 and carried downwind. These droplets exchange heat and water with the air; if they evaporate, they can leave salt and
 organic residues. At high humidity they may persist longer. Spray can therefore coexist with true fog, and a coastal scene can
 contain a near-water spray layer beneath advection fog aloft. [7-10]

 Modeling caution: do not generate sea fog solely from wind speed. Wind and wave state create spray and bubbles; fog requires a
 thermodynamic path to saturation. They should be separate but coupled processes.

6.1 Evaporation does not always mean instant disappearance

Droplet lifetime depends on initial diameter, air temperature, relative humidity, ventilation, and dissolved material. Small
pure-water droplets can shrink rapidly in subsaturated air. Salt or organic residue may remain after most water evaporates. At
higher humidity, droplets may settle into an equilibrium haze-like state or take up water. A visible plume may therefore change
from liquid droplets to nearly invisible residual particles without physically travelling far. [7-9]

6.2 Aerosol source chemistry

Source water matters. Sea spray carries sea salt and a variable organic fraction; freshwater spray has much less sea salt but
can carry dissolved minerals, organic matter, microbes, and suspended solids. Rain splash can mobilize material from a
surface. Once airborne, particles can take up gases, undergo aqueous chemistry while wet, or deposit onto land and water.
Treating all droplets as optically and chemically identical misses this source dependence. [7,8]

7. Fog dispersion and dissipation

Fog is maintained by a balance of cooling or moisture supply, condensation, turbulent transport, radiation, and droplet loss.
Dissipation usually does not mean the droplets “fall out” like rain. More often, the air becomes subsaturated and droplets
evaporate, or mixing replaces a foggy parcel with warmer or drier air. [3,5,6]

7.1 Why wind can either clear or thicken fog

Wind is not a universal fog-removal switch. Stronger wind can mix a saturated surface layer with warm/dry air and dissipate it.
But it can also advect warm moist air over cold water, replenish moisture, or transport an existing fog bank. Gentle turbulence
may help mix moisture from a wet surface through a shallow layer; stronger turbulence may erode it. The air mass and
surface boundary conditions matter as much as wind speed.

7.2 Fog is a moving field, not a solid object

Fog edges are zones where droplet number and liquid water content change. In one location, droplets may evaporate while
new droplets form a short distance away. Visual movement can reflect air transport, changing illumination, or changing droplet
size rather than a coherent body of water moving intact. For simulation, represent fog as a concentration field transported by
wind and turbulence, with local source/sink terms for condensation, evaporation, and deposition.

  Practical chain: air transport + surface heat and moisture fluxes -> local temperature and vapor -> droplet activation/growth ->
  liquid water content and droplet sizes -> light extinction/visibility -> evaporation, deposition, or precipitation.

8. Droplet size, optics, and visibility

Fog is visible because droplets scatter light. Their size and concentration determine how strongly a beam is redirected and
how quickly contrast is lost with distance. A fog bank made of many tiny droplets can have high optical extinction even when
the total liquid mass is modest. Conversely, a spray cloud containing fewer but much larger drops may look different, fall out
faster, and produce different near-field scattering. [4,7,8]

8.1 Visibility and extinction

In a uniform, approximately homogeneous fog, a useful meteorological approximation is the Koschmieder relation:

Visual range: V ~= 3.912 / beta
V is the visibility distance in meters for a 2% contrast threshold, and beta is the atmospheric extinction coefficient in inverse
meters. This approximation is useful for intuition and rendering, but real scenes require care with illumination, background
contrast, nonuniform density, and multiple scattering.

As droplet number or effective size changes, extinction changes. At visible wavelengths, fog droplets are commonly in a size
regime where Mie scattering is important; they are not simply tiny Rayleigh scatterers. For physically consistent models, use a
size distribution (number concentration by diameter), not only one average radius, when fine accuracy is needed.

8.3 White fog, gray fog, and glowing halos

Dense fog often appears gray or white because scattered light arrives from many directions and contrast collapses. Fog
illuminated from behind can glow; side lighting can reveal streaks and local density gradients. Fine droplets can create halos
or coronas around lights under appropriate distributions, while water droplets on nearby lenses or surfaces produce different
optical artifacts. Rendering should separate volume scattering from surface droplets and from lens effects.

9. Fog water, deposition, and environmental effects

 Fog is a multiphase chemical environment rather than pure distilled water suspended in air. Droplets can scavenge soluble
 gases and particles, host aqueous reactions, and deposit material on vegetation, buildings, and soil. When the fog
 evaporates, some nonvolatile components remain as aerosol. The chemistry depends on air pollution, marine salts, organic
 matter, and local biological emissions. [4,7]

 9.1 Fog deposition and cloud forests

 In persistent fog regions, leaves, needles, and branches intercept droplets. The resulting fog drip can add water to
 ecosystems, particularly where rainfall is limited or seasonal. Deposition depends on droplet size, wind, vegetation
 roughness, canopy structure, and whether droplets evaporate after collision. Fog frequency alone does not quantify water
 input; liquid water content, wind, exposure, and collection efficiency also matter.

 9.2 Coastal atmosphere and marine aerosol

 Sea spray transports sodium chloride and a variable amount of organic and biological material into the marine boundary layer.
 As droplets evaporate, they may leave particles that influence aerosol optical properties and later cloud condensation. Some
 spray is transported far from the sea; larger droplets tend to remain nearer the surface. Bubble bursting and wave breaking
 couple ocean chemistry, heat and gas exchange, cloud microphysics, and coastal air quality. [8-10]

 9.3 Freezing fog and ice fog

 Freezing fog is supercooled liquid fog that freezes onto exposed surfaces. Ice fog consists of suspended ice particles and
 occurs in very cold conditions, especially in polar or interior winter environments. Their formation depends on temperatures,
 nuclei, vapor conditions, and local sources such as combustion or open water. Do not treat every subzero fog as an
 ice-crystal field: supercooled liquid droplets can persist below 0 C. [1,2]

   Environmental distinction: fog can be a source of water and dissolved substances through deposition, but it can also transport
   pollutants and salts. Its net ecological effect depends on chemistry, frequency, liquid water content, and the receiving surface.

10.1 Simplified saturation logic

• Compute saturation vapor pressure from temperature and compare it with the actual vapor pressure.
• If RH exceeds 100% in a numerical step, relax supersaturation by condensing vapor into droplets rather than allowing
uncontrolled excess moisture. In a simple model, use a timescale; in a microphysical model, use droplet growth and aerosol
activation.
• If RH falls below 100%, evaporate liquid droplets subject to the available liquid water and a size-dependent evaporation
rate.
• Advect fog-water and spray-droplet fields with wind and mix them with a turbulence term. Add source terms for water-body
fog, waves, falls, jets, and spray impacts.
• Convert the evolving droplet distribution to optical extinction. Do not map visibility directly from RH alone; high RH can occur
without visible fog if liquid droplets are absent.

10.2 A conceptual fog-water budget

  Change in airborne liquid water = condensation growth + injected spray - evaporation - deposition - precipitation loss + net
  transport.

  Change in vapor = surface evaporation and vapor advection - condensation - mixing losses or gains.

  The same water can move between vapor, suspended droplets, surface water, and aerosol residue. Tracking only a “fog density”
  slider skips the mechanism that should create and remove it.

11.1 Quick diagnostic order

• First, locate the source: surface cooling, warm water, rain, waves, a waterfall, or a jet?

• Second, classify the pathway: condensation, mechanical breakup, bubble bursting, or a mixture?

• Third, inspect the air-water contrast: air temperature, water temperature, dew point, RH, wind, and stability.

• Fourth, infer transport and lifetime: droplet size, turbulent mixing, downwind dilution, evaporation, surface collection, and
precipitation.

• Finally, infer the optics: droplet number and size, humidity, lighting, background contrast, and any non-water aerosol
residue.

The visual category is the last step, not the first. Starting from mechanism is more reliable than choosing “fog” because the
plume happens to look white.

 Key takeaways

 • Fog is an airborne droplet field created by saturation and condensation; spray is mechanically broken liquid; foam is gas
 bubbles contained by liquid films.
 • Ground fog is commonly connected to radiative cooling and terrain pooling, but the label describes where it sits rather than
 proving its cause.
 • Cold water can cool moist air into advection fog. Warm water can moisten cold air into steam or evaporation fog. Both can
 occur near the same shoreline under changing conditions.
 • Breaking waves and bubbles make sea-spray aerosols; foam does not simply turn into a fog bank, though bubble bursting
 ejects droplets that may later evaporate or participate in aerosol and cloud processes.
 • Fog disperses through evaporation, mixing, advection, changing surface fluxes, deposition, and sometimes drizzle or
 freezing. Wind can clear, create, or transport fog depending on the air mass and source boundary.
 • For physical simulation, track temperature, vapor, aerosol nuclei, droplet water, droplet size, sources, transport, and optics
 separately enough that each process has a plausible cause.

   Scope note: This is an educational synthesis, not a substitute for aviation, marine, or road-visibility forecasts. Actual
   atmospheric fog and sea-spray production depend on multiscale turbulence and droplet microphysics; several bubble-bursting
   and aerosol-production details remain active research topics.
