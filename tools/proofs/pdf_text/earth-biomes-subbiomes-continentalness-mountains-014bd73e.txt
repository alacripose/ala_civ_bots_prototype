A high-resolution conceptual atlas of climate, continentalness, relief, hydrology, soils, and
ecological patterning across Earth.

Central idea: a biome is not painted onto a map by latitude alone. It emerges from long-term energy and water balance,
modified by distance from the ocean, atmospheric circulation, mountain barriers, landform, substrate, fire, flooding, and
ecological history.

  Use this guide as a practical, broad approximation of global biome and sub-biome diversity. It is intentionally

  global biome.

WHAT THIS ATLAS COVERS

• Climate geography: tropical-to-polar gradients, oceanic versus continental climates, seasonality, monsoons, and
   moisture balance.

• Terrain: mountain belts, windward and leeward slopes, elevation zones, plateaus, basins, valleys, floodplains, dunes, and
   coasts.

• Ecological taxonomy: major terrestrial biomes plus a wide catalog of recognizable sub-biomes and special substrate- or
   water-controlled systems.

• Modeling logic: a repeatable way to connect climate, elevation, slope, drainage, geology, and disturbance when
   classifying real landscapes or building a terrain system.

Classification note: there is no single universally adopted global hierarchy of “biome -> sub-biome”. Different scientific products classify climate,
potential vegetation, ecoregions, land cover, or ecological land units for different purposes. This document treats the hierarchy as a useful synthesis
and flags where categories overlap. See references [1]-[5].

How to use the atlas

Start with the physical controls, then use the biome catalog. The same visible vegetation can arise from different histories,
and similar climates can support different vegetation where fire, soils, grazing, flooding, or geological history differ. A map
label is therefore a summary of a coupled system, not a complete explanation.

1. The climate engine: heat, water, and seasonality

The first-order pattern of terrestrial vegetation is strongly constrained by energy and water. Solar input varies with latitude
and season; atmospheric circulation redistributes heat and moisture; oceans store heat and supply vapor; and terrain
modifies airflow and runoff. Plants respond to growing-season temperature, frost frequency, drought intensity, atmospheric
demand, and the duration of water availability - not only annual averages. [2, 6]

A useful moisture balance is P - ET, where P is precipitation and ET is actual evapotranspiration. For vegetation potential,
PET is also important because it estimates the atmosphere’s capacity to remove water. Two sites with equal annual rainfall
can differ sharply if one has cool summers and low evaporative demand while the other has intense heat, wind, and a long
rainless season. Soil storage and groundwater can further buffer drought.

Why annual averages are insufficient

• Minimum winter temperature can determine whether broadleaf evergreen species survive, even if annual mean
   temperature is mild.

• Rainfall seasonality can separate evergreen forest, seasonal forest, savanna, and thorn scrub at similar annual totals.

• Snowfall stored through winter may support spring growth; rain of the same amount at a different time has a different
   ecological effect.

• Fire, grazing, and flood regimes can maintain a grassland or open woodland where climate alone might also allow more
   trees.

  For a defensible biome assignment, use at least: temperature seasonality, precipitation amount and
  seasonality, evaporative demand, elevation, and one measure of terrain-controlled moisture. Add soils and
  disturbance when the goal is finer than a broad climate-biome map.

2. Continentalness: from maritime margins to inland climates

Continentalness describes the strength of land-interior influence relative to the thermal and moisture buffering of nearby
oceans. Water has high heat capacity and mixes vertically; land surfaces generally heat and cool more rapidly. As a result,
maritime climates often have smaller annual temperature ranges and milder winters, while continental interiors tend to have
larger seasonal extremes. Continentalness is not a simple distance-to-coast rule: prevailing winds, ocean currents,
mountain barriers, latitude, sea ice, and regional circulation can strengthen or weaken the pattern.

Mechanisms that matter

• Seasonal thermal range: inland surfaces commonly experience hotter summers and colder winters than nearby maritime
   settings at comparable latitude and elevation.

• Moisture trajectory: air masses may lose moisture as they travel over land, especially when uplift and precipitation occur
   along intervening ranges. But storm tracks and monsoons can bring moisture far inland.

• Coastal currents: cool eastern-boundary currents often favor stable marine layers and fog along west coasts in
   subtropical dry belts; warm currents can add moisture and alter storm intensity.

• Snow and frozen ground: continental interiors can have long snow seasons or strong seasonal frost, changing growing
   seasons, soil drainage, and spring runoff.

Do not infer biome from continentalness alone. A humid continental forest, a prairie, and a cold desert can all
occur far from oceans because temperature, rainfall, soil storage, fire, and topography interact.

Useful quantitative approach: calculate a continentality index from annual temperature range and latitude only if you need a comparative metric; for
ecological mapping, direct climatologies (monthly temperatures, precipitation, PET, frost days, snow cover) usually preserve more interpretable
information.

3. Mountains: uplift, rain shadows, and ecological
compression

Mountains reorganize climate over short horizontal distances. When moist air is forced upward, it expands and cools;
condensation and precipitation often increase on windward slopes. Air descending on the lee side compresses and warms,
often lowering relative humidity. The strength and location of this contrast depends on wind direction, stability, moisture
content, storm structure, and the range’s orientation and height. Orographic precipitation is complex, so not every mountain
has a neat wet side and dry side. [7, 8]

WINDWARD / MOIST                                                                  LEEWARD / DRIER

Air rises, expands and cools; cloud and precipitation often increase.             Descending air warms; relative humidity often falls.

Why relief changes biome boundaries

• Elevation lowers temperature: temperature commonly decreases with altitude, but the local lapse rate varies with
   humidity, season, inversions, and time of day. Cold-air pooling can make valley floors colder than slopes at night.

• Cloud belts move upslope: windward slopes may intercept cloud water and fog; cloud forests can occur where frequent
   cloud immersion supplements rainfall.

• Snowpack redistributes water: snow accumulates in shaded hollows and high basins, then melts later than lowland
   rain. Snowbeds and meltwater corridors can support plants unlike the surrounding slopes.

• Aspect changes radiation: equator-facing slopes tend to receive more solar energy than pole-facing slopes in
   mid-latitudes. They are often warmer and drier; the direction is reversed between hemispheres.

• Terrain redistributes soil and water: ridges shed water and often have thin, exposed soils; concave hollows collect
   colluvium and moisture; floodplains receive sediment and groundwater.

Mountain effect is more than altitude

The same elevation may hold closed forest on a humid windward slope, open woodland on a sunny aspect, alpine meadow
on a windy ridge, peatland in a poorly drained basin, and bare scree on a steep unstable face. Bedrock affects soil depth
and chemistry; glacial history affects landforms and lakes; avalanche tracks and recurring snow slides create disturbance
corridors. [4, 5, 9]

  A mountain should usually be classified as a stack of ecological zones and terrain facets, not as one “mountain
  biome”. The FAO’s broad mountain-system classes are useful at global scale, but each range contains several
  climates and vegetation types.

4. Landform, soil, drainage, and local mosaics

Climate sets the broad envelope; terrain and substrate decide where water and nutrients are stored, concentrated, or lost. A
regional climate zone can therefore contain unexpectedly wet valley forests, dry rocky ridges, salt flats, bogs, and
grasslands on shallow soils. USGS Global Ecological Land Units explicitly combines bioclimate, landform, lithology, and
land cover; it produced 3,923 mapped ecological land units from 250 m base-resolution inputs, illustrating how much
variation is hidden within broad biome labels. [3]

Soils are not merely a passive layer

Soil texture controls infiltration and water-holding capacity; depth determines rooting volume; organic matter changes
aggregation and storage; salinity imposes osmotic stress; pH and mineralogy affect nutrient availability. Soil age, parent
material, climate, vegetation, and topography interact through time. [10, 11] Two nearby sites can therefore support different
communities despite sharing weather.

Landscape water paths

• Runoff-dominated: steep slopes, crusted surfaces, shallow soils, or intense storms can route water quickly into gullies and
   channels.

• Infiltration-dominated: permeable soils or fractured bedrock can move water downward, sometimes sustaining springs
   and groundwater-dependent vegetation.

• Storage-dominated: deep soils, peat, snowpacks, and alluvial aquifers can buffer dry periods.

• Evapoconcentration: closed basins in arid climates accumulate salts where evaporation exceeds flushing and
   groundwater discharges near the surface.

5. Global biome overview: useful levels of classification

Global biome maps differ because their aims differ. WWF terrestrial ecoregions emphasize distinctive assemblages of
species and communities; Köppen-Geiger maps classify climate; FAO ecological zones summarize broad

system should retain its scale and purpose rather than blend all labels into one supposedly universal list. [1-5, 12]

6. Tropical and subtropical forest systems

Tropical systems are not all rainforest. The length and reliability of the dry season, flooding regime, elevation, and substrate
produce strong structural differences. Warm temperatures allow year-round growth where water and nutrients permit, but
seasonal drought can favor deciduousness, deep roots, fire-resistant traits, and open canopies.

Terrain modifiers in the tropics

• Windward slopes can carry cloud forest above lowland rainforest, while adjacent rain shadows support montane dry forest
   or scrub.

• Flood basins create swamp and seasonally flooded forest inside otherwise terra-firme rainforest regions.

• Nutrient-poor ancient soils can support distinctive heath forest or white-sand vegetation, while young alluvium can support
   more productive forest.

• In savanna mosaics, shallow soils, rock pavements, fire frequency, grazing and waterlogging can keep tree cover low
   despite rainfall that could support forest.

Tropical mountain systems deserve their own elevation and exposure layers; they are not simply a cooler copy of lowland tropical forest. [4, 5]

7. Drylands: deserts, semi-deserts, and xeric shrublands

Drylands occupy climates where precipitation is low or unreliable relative to evaporative demand. Their appearance
depends heavily on substrate and water-routing: sandy dune fields, rocky pavements, salt basins, ephemeral washes, and
mountain-front fans are very different habitats within the same arid region. In arid terrain, the soil-geomorphic template can
strongly predict vegetation patterns. [13]

8. Temperate forests, rainforests, and Mediterranean systems

Temperate biomes occupy many combinations of winter cold, summer heat, precipitation, and seasonality. Distance from
the ocean often separates mild oceanic forest from strongly seasonal continental forest. Mediterranean climates are
unusual because summers are dry while winters are the wetter season; their shrublands and woodlands frequently have
long histories of nutrient limitation and fire. [15]

Why the Mediterranean family is not just “dry forest”

Its defining climatic pattern is usually wet winter and dry summer, unlike summer-rainfall savanna or many continental
steppe systems. Plants must cope with summer water deficit while often exploiting winter moisture. Fire regimes,
nutrient-poor soils, and regional evolutionary histories produce similar-looking but compositionally distinct shrublands:
chaparral, maquis, matorral, fynbos, and kwongan are analogues, not exact ecological equivalents. [15]

9. Grasslands, savannas, shrublands, and open woodlands

These are not merely failed forests. Open biomes can be maintained by climate, seasonal drought, fire, herbivory, shallow
or nutrient-poor soils, waterlogging, cold, or interactions among these factors. In many regions, removing fire or large
herbivores changes woody cover; elsewhere rainfall and soil constraints keep forests from closing even without frequent
fire.

13. Continental transects: North America and Eurasia

These examples show how large-scale patterns arise and why biome boundaries curve, fragment, and interlock. They are
schematic, not literal west-to-east or north-to-south transects for every latitude.

North America: Pacific margin to continental interior

Pacific maritime coast: mild temperatures and strong wet-season precipitation support temperate rainforest in the wettest
coastal belts; conifer forests grade inland with declining ocean influence and orographic barriers. Coast Ranges /
Cascades / Sierra Nevada: windward slopes are often wetter, with strong snowpack and elevation bands. Intermontane
basins: rain shadows support sagebrush-like shrub steppe, cold desert, salt basins and dry grassland. Great Plains: a
broad moisture gradient from moister tallgrass toward mixed-grass and shortgrass steppe grades westward, influenced by
continentality, topography and fire. Eastern forests: humid conditions support deciduous and mixed forest; southward,
warm humid forest; northward, boreal forest and tundra. [1, 2, 7]

North American mountain mosaics

• Olympic and coastal ranges: maritime moisture, cloud belts, temperate rain forest, snowfields, and dry lee-side areas over
   short distances.

• Rocky Mountains: foothill grassland or shrubland -> montane forest -> subalpine forest/krummholz -> alpine tundra;
   aspect and snowpack create wet hollows and dry ridges.

• Great Basin ranges: basin-and-range relief isolates mountain “islands” of woodland and montane forest above desert
   valleys; north-facing slopes and springs can support mesic pockets.

• Appalachians: elevation and aspect create cooler coves, rich hardwood forests, high-elevation spruce-fir patches and
   exposed heath balds in regional contexts.

Eurasia: Atlantic margin to interior Asia

Western Atlantic margin: oceanic temperatures and regular moisture support broadleaf forest, mixed forest and heath
mosaics. Central/eastern Europe: more seasonal continental forest and steppe transition. Central Asia: dry steppe, cold
desert, mountain rain shadows and internally drained basins dominate large areas. Siberia: boreal forest grades into larch
woodland, tundra and polar desert northward; permafrost and fire shape drainage and succession. South and East Asian
monsoon regions: seasonal rainfall drives moist and dry forests, subtropical evergreen forests and major mountain
gradients. [2, 3, 14]

  Eurasia demonstrates both latitude and continentalness: moving north cools the climate, while moving inland
  strengthens seasonal extremes and often increases aridity; mountain arcs then create strong local exceptions.

14. Continental transects: Africa, South America, and
Australia

Africa: equatorial wet zone, savanna belts, and deserts

The Congo Basin supports tropical moist forest, with flooded forest and swamp mosaics in lowlands. Moving outward from
the equatorial humid core, seasonal forests grade into savannas and dry woodlands where the dry season lengthens and
fire becomes more influential. The Sahel forms a variable semi-arid transition north of wetter savannas, then the Sahara
spans multiple desert expressions including sand seas, gravel plains, mountains and oases. Southern Africa combines
savannas, Highveld grasslands, the Namib fog-desert margin, Karoo shrublands and the Cape fynbos region. East African
highlands add montane forest, afroalpine vegetation and sharp rain-shadow contrasts.

South America: Amazon, Andes, and dry east/west contrasts

The Amazon lowlands contain extensive rainforest and flood-pulse ecosystems. The Andes generate a striking west-to-east
and elevation mosaic: Pacific-facing dry regions, high puna and other alpine systems, cloud forest on moist slopes, and
Amazon-facing foothill rainforest. Central Brazil’s Cerrado is a savanna and woodland mosaic affected by soils, fire and
rainfall seasonality; the Caatinga is seasonally dry thorn scrub; the Pampas are temperate grasslands; the Gran Chaco
includes dry forests, scrub and wetlands; Patagonia contains steppe and cold desert in the lee of the Andes. Southern
Chile’s maritime exposure supports temperate rain forest in wet belts. [1, 7]

Australia: strong aridity, old landscapes, and coastal exceptions

Much of the interior is arid or semi-arid, with spinifex grasslands, mulga-like shrublands, salt lakes, sand ridges, rocky
ranges and episodic drainage. Northern Australia supports tropical savannas with monsoon seasonality. Wetter eastern and
southeastern margins include eucalypt forests, temperate forests, alpine zones in the southeast, and subtropical/tropical
rainforest pockets where moisture and topography allow. Southwestern Australia hosts Mediterranean-type shrublands and
woodlands including kwongan; Tasmania has cool temperate rainforest and other temperate systems.

Islands and archipelagos

Islands can create sharp elevation belts, cloud forests, windward/lee patterns, and endemism over small distances.
Volcanic islands may have young porous substrates, geothermal zones, lava fields, and strong soil-age gradients. Oceanic
isolation and local history can make their species assemblages unlike those of continental regions with similar climate.

Comparative lesson

Similar climate envelopes do not create identical biomes everywhere. The Cape fynbos, Californian chaparral, central
Chilean matorral, Mediterranean maquis and southwestern Australian kwongan share some climate and plant-functional
traits, but their species histories, soils and fire regimes differ. Use the climatic/terrain label to describe physical conditions;
use ecoregion or floristic information when species identity matters. [15]

16. A practical ruleset for assigning biomes to terrain

For a GIS map, simulation, or procedural terrain system, a robust approach is hierarchical: classify broad climate first, then
modify it by relief and water routing, then apply soil and disturbance overlays. Avoid using altitude as a direct biome lookup
without latitude and moisture context.

Suggested classification pipeline

1. Climate envelope

Use monthly temperature and precipitation, coldest month, growing-season length, precipitation seasonality, PET or aridity
index, snow fraction and frost frequency. Derive broad thermal/moisture domain.

2. Continentalness and circulation

Estimate maritime influence, seasonal temperature range, prevailing windward direction, storm-track exposure, monsoon
seasonality and coastal-current context where relevant.

3. Topographic position

Compute elevation, slope, aspect, curvature, roughness, topographic wetness, distance to drainage, local relief, valley
depth and ridge exposure.

4. Orographic correction

Where possible, use precipitation and temperature fields corrected for topography; distinguish windward uplift from lee-side
rain shadows. Do not assume every side is simply wet/dry.

5. Water-routing layer

Track runoff accumulation, flood recurrence, seasonal wetness, groundwater discharge, lakes, snowmelt paths and closed
basins. Apply riparian, floodplain, wetland and salt-basin overlays.

6. Substrate / soil layer

Use lithology, soil texture/depth, drainage class, pH/salinity, parent material and soil age if available. These modify
forest/grassland/shrubland outcomes and generate edaphic specialists.

7. Potential vegetation class

Assign the broad biome and candidate sub-biome that fits the climate envelope, then test whether tree cover is plausible
given fire, cold, waterlogging, soil and disturbance.

8. Disturbance and land-use state

Apply fire regime, grazing/herbivory, storm damage, flood regime, avalanches, human land use and succession. Keep
potential vegetation separate from present land cover.

9. Ecotones and uncertainty

Blend or probabilistically assign where climate envelopes overlap. Preserve confidence scores and multiple factors rather
than forcing every transition into a hard line.

Potential vegetation versus current land cover

A climate- and terrain-based map usually estimates what vegetation could persist under specified environmental conditions.
A contemporary land-cover map records what is actually present after human land use, disturbance and succession. A
modern agricultural field can sit within a temperate grassland biome; plantation forest can occupy a rainforest or savanna
region; urban land can hide the local hydrologic and soil system. Do not interpret current cover as the natural biome without
evidence.

Uncertainty is structural, not just measurement error

• Global classifications use different variable sets and boundary rules, and their maps do not always agree. The
   biome-inventory literature documents divergence among global products. [12]

• Climate-only classifications often miss substrate-controlled vegetation, wetlands, and terrain microclimates.

• A single “mountain” category hides huge differences between windward forest, lee desert, alpine meadow, and glacier
   terrain.

• Ecological communities reflect history and available species as well as current climate, so one global rule cannot predict
   every local assemblage.

  For high-fidelity mapping, retain both the best label and the evidence behind it: climate drivers, terrain position,
  soil/water factors, and confidence. When uncertainty is high, store two plausible sub-biomes or an ecotone
  instead of inventing false precision.

18. Glossary and reference map

TERM                          WORKING DEFINITION
Biome                         A broad ecological formation, commonly associated with climate and dominant vegetation structure, though definitions vary.
Sub-biome                     A practical subdivision of a biome; not a universally standardized formal rank.
Ecoregion                     A geographically distinct area characterized by a particular assemblage of communities and species.
Ecological land unit (ELU)    An integrated unit produced by combining physical setting and land-cover information, such as bioclimate, landform, lithology
                              and land cover.
Continentalness               Degree of inland versus maritime climate influence, often expressed by seasonal temperature range and moisture patterns.
Orographic precipitation      Precipitation caused or enhanced by air being forced to rise over topography.
Rain shadow                   A region of reduced precipitation in the lee of a mountain range relative to windward exposure, under suitable circulation
                              conditions.
Potential evapotranspiration  An estimate of atmospheric demand for water from a vegetated surface if water supply were not limiting.
(PET)
Aridity index                 A ratio or index comparing precipitation with evaporative demand; definitions differ by product.
Azonal ecosystem              A system whose local control (such as flooding, salinity or substrate) overrides the broad zonal climate-vegetation pattern.
Edaphic                       Relating to soil conditions such as texture, depth, chemistry, drainage and nutrient supply.
Ecotone                       A transition area between ecological systems, sometimes gradual and sometimes sharply bounded.
Treeline                      The upper or poleward limit of tree growth, varying with climate, exposure, species and disturbance.
Krummholz                     Stunted or wind-shaped woody growth near treeline, often bent or shrub-like due to exposure.
Fellfield                     Open alpine or arctic vegetation with much exposed ground, rock and sparse low plants.
Potential vegetation          Vegetation expected under the environmental conditions specified by a model, often excluding current human land use.
Land cover                    The surface cover actually present, such as forest, grass, crop, bare ground, water or built area.
